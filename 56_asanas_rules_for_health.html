A Brief Life Sketch of Dr. V. V. Ramaraju

Dr. Vegiraju Venkata Ramaraju was born on July 2, 1913, in Marripudi village of Bapatla taluk, Guntur district, a place known as a sacred land of yajnas and rituals. He was the youngest son of Sri Vegiraju Kotamraju and Adeyyamma, who were deeply spiritual. At the age of eight, he stopped eating meat, saying it is not natural human food, and with kindness toward all living beings he even convinced elders around him to give it up.
He had great love for education. But due to the conditions of that time, after finishing school and receiving teacher training in a British-run school, he worked as a teacher and supported the growth of many nearby schools. Later, on the call of his elder brother, Vaidyaraju Vegiraju Krishnamraju, he left teaching and entered Prakriti Chikitsa (Nature Cure). He was then 21 years old. Sri Krishnamraju was a remarkable person, training many youth in stick-fighting and sword displays and turning them into strong physical artists. In Nature Cure he was unmatched, and in Delhi his Sanskrit talks attracted not only common people but also leaders like Gandhiji, Vinoba, Purushottamdas Tandon, and Jamnalal Bajaj.
Inspired by those teachings, Dr. Ramaraju worked in the rural Nature Cure movement and treated many difficult chronic illnesses. In one village, an officer was left outside assuming he would die. Dr. Ramaraju examined him, brought him inside, gave an enema, cleaned his bowels, and bathed him. Within an hour the man began speaking and drinking milk. Villagers were shocked and said some young yogi had come and saved him by magic. But Dr. Ramaraju said humbly: this is not magic or trickery. I only follow an apakwa aaharam (uncooked natural diet, mainly raw foods and fruits) and gave proper Nature Cure cleansing.
In those days, when cholera spread, whole villages would flee, leaving bodies behind. At that time Dr. Ramaraju encouraged youth, had large pits dug, and ensured the dead were buried daily. Even British officials were surprised to see this courage and service.
Living only on uncooked food, that is raw foods and fruits, he traveled about 3,800 miles (over 6,000 km) by bicycle and met many great people. Meeting Mahatma Gandhi was a key moment. On that day public meeting time was over, but after reading a small note from Ramaraju, Gandhi called him in and asked why a young man was living on raw food. Ramaraju replied: this is my elder brother's instruction, and my experiment is to show the strength of natural food. Gandhi blessed him, saying he was doing good work and should not stop.
Dr. Ramaraju deeply remembered a Sanskrit verse: "Na tvaham kaamaye raajyam, na svargam na punarbhavam - kaamaye duhkha-taptaanaam praaninaam arti-naashanam." Its meaning is: I do not desire a kingdom, heaven, or rebirth; I only desire the power to remove the suffering of living beings in pain. He held this ideal throughout life, giving free treatment and food to the poor. From 1936 to 1942, for seven years, he lived only on uncooked natural food and demonstrated its value.
His elder brother established Nature Cure ashrams in Bapatla and in the Nakirekallu forest area. Their experiences in running them were remarkable. They often said two things: 1. Hunger can be deceptive. 2. Weakness can also be deceptive. About fifty years ago, this was stated as a tested truth. Once, a tribal patient with severe vomiting and diarrhea was left at the ashram, almost speechless and immobile. Treatment started. One night loud disturbance was heard near his hut, and people rushed there, fearing a wild bear. But the patient was on the bed. By morning, a seven-foot cobra was found dead. Days later, after recovery, he said he had killed it and then collapsed again. Reflecting on such events, Dr. Ramaraju concluded that hunger and weakness are often strongly influenced by the mind. Between 1936 and 1946, amid many such experiences, he spread awareness of Nature Cure across villages in Bapatla taluk.
In 1946, his elder brother Krishnamraju established a Nature Cure center in Bhimavaram, West Godavari district, and invited him there as a physician. Dr. Ramaraju took charge of operations, gained deep practical knowledge of Nature Cure, and began sharing his experience widely. Many notable people took treatment from him, from film personality L. V. Prasad to members of the Bajaj family. He named his younger son after his elder brother, gave him five and a half years of Nature Cure training, and handed over center responsibilities in 1987. Even at age 89, he conducted yoga camps, taught yoga to 1,200 revenue staff and 140 PETs in the district, and served patients from Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Odisha. Such work at that age was truly extraordinary.
Many people asked him to write books from his experience. He would say: a book can guide, but cannot itself deliver the final result. If people imitate treatment from a book and face harm, the science itself gets blamed. Still, on insistence from friends and family, he wrote books explaining not just experience but also the fundamentals of nature and how to apply them. He never liked publicity. When newspapers, TV, or radio approached him, he said he wanted people's acceptance, not personal promotion.

1. The Secret of Asanas

  • When the body stays steady and comfortable - that is an asana
  • Asanas improve health - they create harmony between body and mind
  • Stored wastes are removed - gland function improves
  • When organs work in coordination - life energy (prana shakti) flows properly
  • When you practice asanas, the mind stays alert - the body becomes healthy
An asana means keeping the body still and comfortable in a posture. Many people think doing asanas is just like physical exercise. Like exercise, asanas do affect the body deeply, but asanas are not done only with the body. Every person is a combination of body, mind, and awareness. The main aim of asanas is to bring these three into balance. If asanas were only body movements, we could do them while thinking about home, office, or anything else. But while doing asanas, full attention must stay on the body. Other thoughts should not enter the mind. We should observe mental state, breathing pattern, and muscle response. Only then is the asana done correctly. In normal exercise, this kind of inner attention is not required, so its benefit is only partial.
When a person is physically and mentally unwell, they cannot do life’s work with energy, and they cannot achieve what they want. Only a healthy body and mind can work with strength and efficiency. Our ancestors understood this thousands of years ago and developed asanas as a method for well-being. In old times, before modern medicines, they studied how the body could stay healthy by using its own natural power. The methods they found are still useful today. Modern medical systems are newer, but asanas have been refined over thousands of years as a clear method for physical health, mental health, and spiritual growth.
People in old times and people today may live differently, but the need for asanas is the same. Asanas improve health and also help higher awareness. For people living with pain, illness, and mental stress, that higher state may feel far away. First, we must come out of physical and mental suffering. Asanas help that directly. They loosen joints, strengthen muscles, improve circulation to organs, and remove toxins and waste buildup. They give natural massage to internal organs, create body-mind coordination, and provide mental rest. Body and mind are inseparable. When physical health improves fully, mental change also follows.
Mental anxiety and physical weakness both cause many diseases. The body has a natural healing power. It can prevent disease through its own defense system, called immunity. Asanas support this immune strength. When waste accumulates in the body, harmful bacteria and toxins can grow. Asanas help clear these wastes. If major organs like heart, lungs, and kidneys do not function well, disease appears. Asanas help these organs work better and stronger. Many problems also come when nerves do not function properly. Asanas strengthen the nerves and improve nerve signaling to all organs by reducing pressure on them. They also protect us from heavy mental stress, which is a root cause of many illnesses.
Our body has endocrine glands that produce hormones. These hormones regulate organ functions, and some also affect the mind. Mental states also affect how these glands work. For example, the hormone adrenaline rises in anger, fear, or anxiety. Then heartbeat increases, breathing becomes faster, and muscles tighten. Adrenaline also helps us react in danger, such as jumping aside suddenly when a vehicle comes toward us.
Growth in children depends on thyroid hormones. In women, hormones are essential for ovulation and pregnancy. Hormones control many processes in many forms - including which organ should work fast and which should slow down. For health, each endocrine gland should release the right amount of hormone at the right time. If this balance is disturbed, both physical and mental changes occur. One major benefit of asanas is better endocrine balance. These glands can weaken due to overstrain, poor body use, mental stress, poor nerve signaling, and low oxygen-rich blood supply. Asanas gradually correct these issues and help glands return to proper function. Health is possible only when all organs work in coordination. Without that coordination, body efficiency drops and disease appears. Asanas improve this coordination.
There is an invisible energy field around the body, which can be called an energy sheath. In yoga, this is called pranamaya kosha. This concept was also discussed in scientific circles through methods like Kirlian photography. This energy moves through pathways inside and outside the body. In yoga, these pathways are called nadis (energy channels). When these paths are blocked, or when too much energy gathers in one place, mental and physical disturbances may arise. Asanas help prana shakti flow smoothly through these channels and support good health.
Asanas change the breathing pattern. Fast and uneven breathing is a sign of physical and mental stress. With asanas, breathing becomes slower, deeper, and more even. This naturally brings mental calmness.
During asana practice, the mind stays alert. The mind usually runs everywhere, but in asanas it should remain on the body. If this attention is absent, it cannot be called complete asana practice. Even if posture looks good physically, benefit is limited when the mind is not involved. Keeping attention on breath during asanas prevents worries for that period. Though this may seem temporary, over time it creates deep mental change.
The body reflects mental states. In anger, shoulders and chest rise. In sadness, we sigh. In anxiety, breathing and speech become shaky and fast. In happiness, the whole body feels light and free. So the body shows the state of mind. Asanas strengthen both mind and body, reduce pain and distress, and shape both into a healthier and more balanced condition.

2. What Is an Asana?

  • An asana means a motionless posture - it must be done with proper method
  • If posture-hold is done wrongly - benefit is almost nothing
  • Understand the three stages - only then practice asanas properly
An asana means placing the body in a posture and staying still. Every asana has three stages. Only when all three are done properly is it a complete asana. These stages are: action stage, asana-hold stage, and rest stage. Let us understand them briefly.
1. First stage - Action stage (how to enter the posture): Every asana must be done in a sequence. Following that sequence is the action stage. This means entering the posture correctly and preparing the body to move into the second stage. If we enter and exit immediately without moving into stage two, there is no benefit. That is not full asana practice. No matter how many postures are done like this, benefit remains low. For the first stage to give result, the second stage must follow.
2. Second stage - Asana-hold stage (steady, motionless hold): This is the most important stage in asana practice. Here, the body stays still in the same posture for as long as possible. Most of the benefit depends on how long you stay and how focused your mind is during this stage. If this stage is done correctly, around 60 to 70 percent of the benefit comes here.
If you only do stage one and remove the posture without stage two, it is not called a full asana. If only one stage is done, it is closer to exercise. For example, in Surya Namaskara there are 12 postures. Even if all 12 are done in sequence, it is not the same as full asana practice because posture-hold and rest are usually not maintained for each position. So Surya Namaskara and asana practice give different results.
Many people do the first stage correctly, but come out too fast from the second stage. Some people keep moving forward and backward while in posture like exercise, instead of staying steady. Then it is not complete asana. Expected results do not come. Instead of 60 to 70 percent benefit, only 20 to 30 percent may come. If this key stage is missed, asana and exercise become almost the same.
3. Third stage - Rest stage (full rest for the worked body): After holding the asana steadily, full rest must be given. Rest stage is not only rest - it also gives 30 to 40 percent of the total benefit. Sit or lie down for 1 to 2 minutes until breath and body return to normal. In this stage, body, mind, muscles, and nerves relax deeply. If you skip proper rest and move to the next asana, full benefit is lost.
Many practitioners do many postures without understanding this method fully. They do not give enough importance to hold stage and rest stage. Then they feel asanas are not giving results. In this way, no one gets full benefit. Because results are weak, many stop midway and shift to walking, exercise, aerobics, or gym routines.
To fill this gap and build complete understanding, I am explaining these three stages clearly and giving priority to them while teaching fifteen important asanas. If practiced correctly, these fifteen are enough to give full benefit. To gain full physical and mental results, all three stages must be completed. Practice this way for some time and you will understand what true asana practice means. In later chapters, these stages are explained in more detail. Read carefully, understand well, and begin practice with the right attitude.

3. Changes in Blood Circulation Due to Asanas

  • Every part must get enough blood - then organ performance stays good
  • The heart is like a hand pump - it sends about 5 liters per minute
  • When circulation is good in muscles - waste removal in cells improves
  • Muscles need proper blood supply - asanas improve this support
  • Through asanas blood gets redirected - compression in organs gets relieved
For all organs to function properly, they must receive enough blood continuously. Only then does organ performance stay strong. So first, let us understand how blood helps the body, what its functions are, and how much blood the heart supplies.
Functions of blood in the body
1. It carries oxygen from the lungs to cells. It carries carbon dioxide from cells back to the lungs for removal.
2. Digested nutrients from the small intestine - such as monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids, glycerol, vitamins, and minerals - are absorbed into blood. Blood then delivers them to all organs.
3. It carries waste products from cells to excretory organs. Some wastes are processed in the liver and then made ready for removal. Protein metabolism wastes like urea, uric acid, and creatinine are carried to kidneys and removed through urine.
4. Blood carries enzymes and hormones from where they are produced to the organs that need them.
5. White blood cells destroy harmful microbes entering the blood. The body forms antibodies and antitoxins to protect immunity.
6. When injury happens, blood clotting helps prevent excess blood loss.
7. It helps regulate body temperature. In heat, it supports heat loss through sweat. In cold, surface vessels narrow to conserve internal heat.
The heart supplies blood to all parts of the body. Like a hand pump pushing water each stroke, each heartbeat pushes blood into vessels. Normally, the human heart beats about 70-72 times per minute. Each beat sends around 60-70 milliliters of blood. So, at rest, it supplies around 4.5 to 5 liters per minute. Even without doing work, this supply continues.
When we work hard, exercise, or think intensely, this normal supply is not enough. Active parts spend more energy and need more oxygen. To provide that, lungs and heart must work more. Then the heart beats faster and stronger and sends more blood to active organs. Less active organs receive less blood according to need. Important organs like heart muscle, lungs, brain, kidneys, and liver get priority supply.
People doing physical work use most muscles regularly. But those sitting in offices, or those living without active work, do not use all muscles. Good blood flow comes only when muscles are used. At rest, many capillaries stay closed, and only some remain open. They open and close in shifts. In resting muscle, blood flow is low; in active muscle it rises sharply. When demand rises, more capillaries open automatically.
Only with proper circulation do cellular waste processes work well. Carbon dioxide from cells is carried to lungs through blood. So muscles stay healthy when they are made to work. In asanas, selected muscles work for some time and then relax again. In each posture, different muscle groups get this effect.
For example, when we clench the fist, forearm muscles tighten and the hand may look compressed. This happens because veins carrying blood back to the heart are pressed. So blood moves back faster. If the fist remains tight, soon the hand may look swollen. Why? Because pressure builds from incoming blood, capillaries open, and blood demand rises in that area. When we open the fist, blood rushes in strongly. This is greater than usual flow. With capillary opening, cell respiration improves and carbon dioxide release also improves.
In asanas, not only muscles but also internal organs in specific areas receive pressure. During the hold, circulation in those compressed areas may reduce briefly, causing discomfort or pain. Once the posture is released, blood flow to that part rises above normal. In this way, asanas help redirect blood to different areas without forcing the heart excessively. If each organ gets this improved flow daily, its capacity improves. Waste products clear better. Just as fresh blood reaches all parts, used blood also returns to the heart more freely. This supports heart efficiency and reduces strain. So regular asana practice helps keep the heart healthy while supplying organs properly.

4. Difference Between Asanas and Exercise

  • Why this strong focus only on exercise - often asanas give deeper benefit
  • When lactic acid rises in blood - carbon dioxide release also rises
  • In asanas, deep breathing develops - oxygen needs of cells are met better
  • Heavy exercise causes sweating - blood water content may reduce
  • Asanas offer major support - without overloading the heart
  • When attention stays on breath - mind becomes steady and body gets rest
In today’s society, many people know exercise better than asanas. Exercise has wider popularity. One reason is fewer teachers for asanas. Another is that even trained people do not always teach others actively. Western influence is strong, and since they emphasize exercise, people here also follow the same path. Doctors also often recommend exercise, and training centers for exercise are common. So people show more interest in exercise.
Even people who know a little about asanas may think asanas are only simple bends without effort, while exercise moves the full body, causes sweat, and feels harder - so they assume it gives better results. But in reality, asanas can provide deeper benefits to the body, and also give mental calmness. To understand clearly, we should compare what changes happen in the body during exercise and during asanas.
Breathing changes during exercise: Breathing and circulation respond based on oxygen demand. At rest, breathing is slow and deep. During work, breathing rate rises. The harder the work, the higher the breathing rate. Normally lungs take in around 6-8 liters of air per minute, but in heavy effort this can rise to 50-100 liters. Oxygen use at rest is much lower than during intense activity. So during exercise, oxygen demand rises sharply.
When oxygen falls in cells and carbon dioxide rises, more oxygen is drawn from blood into tissues. During heavy muscular work, lactic acid production increases. Lactic acid in blood can rise many times above resting levels. This also increases carbon dioxide production. Then lungs must work harder to remove it. Much of the extra oxygen taken in is used to clear this carbon dioxide load, and cells may still feel energy shortage. That is why exercise can cause fatigue and tiredness.
Breathing changes due to asanas: In asanas, breathing speed does not rise sharply, and lungs are not forced to overwork. Unlike heavy exercise, muscles are not pushed beyond limit in the same way. Energy is used, but large lactic acid surge and excess carbon dioxide production usually do not occur. Breathing stays slow during the hold. After release, a few deep breaths happen naturally. These deep breaths supply more oxygen effectively. So oxygen needs are met without overstraining lungs.
Circulation changes during exercise: We saw that cells need more oxygen during exercise. To carry this extra oxygen, blood supply must rise and heart must work harder. Heart rate may rise greatly, and blood pumped per beat also increases. Sweating reduces water in blood, red cell concentration shifts, and blood characteristics change under stress. Only then can the body carry enough oxygen for heavy demand. This shows how much extra load heart and lungs handle in exercise. After hard effort, it can take 20-30 minutes or more to return to normal.
Circulation changes in asanas: In exercise, extra blood reaches muscles mainly because the heart works harder. In asanas, increased circulation to muscles and organs can happen without overloading the heart. During hold, blood flow in some parts slows because of compression. On release, those parts receive increased flow. Thus normal blood is redistributed through different postures and then returns to balance. This helps tissues use oxygen better and recover strength.
Changes in mental state: Exercise can make the body active and may give temporary excitement, but it does not always give deep calmness. During exercise, attention is mostly on movement. Later it becomes automatic. Mind and body are not always connected consciously. Also, faster breathing can make the mind restless.
Asanas benefit the mind as much as the body. During asanas, attention stays on specific body parts, and in rest stage it stays on breath. This makes the mind stable and quieter. Deep breathing improves oxygen supply to the brain and supports balanced life energy. After completing all asanas, Shavasana gives deep relaxation to all muscles, and the brain also gets full rest. When brain rests fully, mind becomes calm. That is why after yoga asana practice people often feel renewed energy in both body and mind.

5. Breathing State While Doing Asanas

  • Have doubts about breath? - leave that part to the body, friend
  • In asanas, enough air comes naturally - do not force breathing
  • Keep your mind on the posture - leave breathing to the body
When doing asanas, many people keep worrying: how to inhale in this posture, how to exhale, and whether they are breathing correctly. Proper explanation on this is often missing, so people practice with confusion. Some are told to inhale while entering, others to exhale while entering, hold the breath in posture, and again inhale or exhale while releasing. Many teachers use this method.
With this approach, posture-hold lasts only as long as breath can be held - usually around 20 to 30 seconds. Then the person must release due to breath need. But asana benefit depends on how long we stay still. Benefit usually starts after the first 10 to 20 seconds and keeps increasing gradually until we reach the point where we truly need to release. If breath is forced or held unnecessarily, we exit early and lose full benefit. Breath control in this strict way may be useful in one or two specific cases, but if done in every asana, full benefit is not achieved.
People are often told to inhale while lifting chest, exhale while bending forward, and hold breath while lifting legs or body. But if we practice the posture correctly with attention, the body itself decides how much air is needed, when to inhale, when to exhale, and when to hold naturally. Even without planning, inhalation happens naturally while lifting chest or waist, and exhalation happens naturally while bending forward.
Also, while holding an asana, abdominal muscles, chest muscles, and lungs may be partially compressed - sometimes by 50 to 90 percent depending on posture. In such moments, we cannot breathe in or out forcefully anyway. The body receives the right amount of air based on the posture. So it is better to leave breathing to the body and focus on staying still in the posture for longer. Let us understand this with an example.
Take Paschimottanasana as an example - sitting with legs stretched and bending forward without bending knees. Some people are told to exhale fully, bend, touch knees, and hold for 10 to 20 seconds. During those seconds, stretch in leg nerves, compression in abdominal muscles, and spinal bend begin to develop. But because breath feels insufficient, they release early. Then they repeat again one or two times. So benefit starts and stops repeatedly, without reaching full depth. With daily repetition like this, progress stays limited.
Now leave breath control aside and hold with head near knees as long as possible. Because the chest is deeply bent, lungs may be partially compressed, but the remaining airflow usually supports basic needs. Stay until nerves and muscles reach their working limit and the body clearly asks for release. Then real stretching and release happen. This may be 20-30 seconds at first, or 1-2 minutes later with practice.
After release, lungs naturally take deep breaths without conscious effort. If you stay fully for your true capacity, repeating the same posture many times is not needed. As practice improves, holding time should gradually increase. If you keep releasing early because of breath fear, progress stops at that same level.
So while doing any asana, keep attention on correct method and posture - not on forcing breath. Do not keep thinking, "air is ending, I must release now," unless the body truly signals the limit. The longer you can stay still with awareness, the better the benefit. To receive true asana benefit, everyone should remember this point clearly.

6. How Long Should Each Asana Be Held?

  • Stay as long as you can - only then benefit becomes strong
  • For good asana-hold - your body condition must support it
  • First learn how to do the posture - then gradually increase time
  • How long is not the only key - staying steady is the right way
  • The longer the right hold, the better the comfort - number of postures is secondary
Usually, 20 or 30 people learn asanas together under a teacher. In the beginning, everyone enters and exits at the same time. This is fine while learning and building habit. But even after 10 to 20 days, many continue the same pattern: entering, exiting with others, and holding for the same fixed time. After 1 or 2 months, they continue this at home too. They follow exactly the seconds or minutes taught in class.
Some teachers give fixed time lists for every asana. Many books also give such time charts. But if we follow fixed timings blindly, full benefit does not come. The right hold time is not decided by the teacher or by someone else - each person must decide for themselves. It depends on body condition, the specific asana, and practice level. The body itself tells how long to stay and when to release. The body's decision should become our decision. Staying in the posture as long as the body truly allows is the best way to get complete benefit.
1. Based on body condition: Body condition differs from person to person. Someone used to physical work can hold differently. Someone with a sedentary life responds differently. Overweight people and very lean people also respond differently. So all bodies do not react the same way in a posture. One person may feel strong discomfort quickly, another may not feel much even after 1 or 2 minutes. If one fixed time is forced for everyone, one person suffers too much, another gets half effect, and another may not even begin to feel the real work. Releasing before the body asks to release gives poor result. So a good habit is to stay as long as your body genuinely allows.
2. Based on the asana: The same asana may come easily to one person, partly to another, and only halfway to another. If everyone is given the same hold time, responses differ, so results differ. For comparable benefit, each person should stay in that asana as long as they can properly hold it. The correct method is to see how far your body cooperates in that posture and hold accordingly.
3. Based on habit and practice: In the first 1 or 2 months, hold time is usually short. With regular daily practice, hold time naturally increases. For example, a knee-touching forward-bend may feel difficult for even 10-15 seconds at first. After 4 months, 1-2 minutes may feel manageable. After 7-8 months, even 2-3 minutes may become easier. This is how the body adapts.
If you always stop at the same fixed time, progress stops. For example, if you always hold only 30 seconds because of habit, result becomes limited. Hold time should grow with practice. One fixed time for all people and all periods is not the right method.
For beginners, during the learning phase, each asana should be held only briefly - around 5 to 10 seconds at first. If the body is overstrained suddenly, the first week may bring strong pain and difficulty in moving around. That can reduce interest in practice. So during the first week, short entry and release prepare muscles, nerves, and organs for asanas. From the second week onward, major beginner pain reduces. Then hold time should be guided by body response and internal signals.
After entering full posture, remain still. During this stage, internal changes happen: stretch in muscles, pressure effects, breath adjustments, and different sensations like pull, compression, heaviness, strain, or pain. Where these sensations appear, there the asana is working. Stay as long as the body can tolerate properly. As time passes, discomfort may rise until the body clearly asks for release and rest. Release at that point.
If your body asks to release in 5 or 10 seconds, do that. Do not feel low because someone else stayed 30 seconds. Never compare with others. Also, do not release just because others released, if your body can continue safely. Close your eyes, observe inner changes, and decide your own timing. The key is not a fixed number of seconds; the key is steady, aware holding for your true capacity.
Just as all vegetables do not cook in the same time, all bodies cannot hold asanas for the same time. Based on your body condition, health, and practice level, stay as long as your body allows in each posture. This is a core secret of asanas. Entering and exiting is not the main thing; holding correctly is the main thing. Doing many asanas is less important than doing each asana correctly. Even with fewer asanas, higher benefit comes when hold quality is good. Practice with this awareness and you will get better results.

7. What If Pain Comes in Asana Posture?

  • Where pain appears, effect is happening - when pain rises, body asks for rest
  • After full asana-hold - proper rest stage must follow
  • Do easier asanas first - difficult ones later
  • Do not rush to repeat many times - that gives little benefit
  • Observe pain in asana - if nothing is felt, effect may be poor
When no organ comes into attention, it usually means all parts are comfortable. But when discomfort appears in an organ, the mind immediately goes there. For example, after a very heavy oily meal, attention goes to the stomach. If bowel movement is not smooth, attention stays on the lower abdomen. In the same way, before asana practice we usually do not notice specific internal parts, but in asana-hold attention shifts to the body.
During asana-hold, some areas are compressed, some stretched, circulation decreases in some places and increases in others. These internal changes may be felt as pain, tingling, heaviness, pressure, or discomfort. By observing where sensation is strongest, we can understand where the asana is working most. Usually pain gradually increases while holding. If a certain area hurts more, that posture is likely helping that area specifically. Rising tolerable pain can be a sign of active effect. Stay while it remains bearable, keep attention there, and observe stages clearly: start of pain, increase, stronger increase, and final limit where rest is needed. At that limit, release and enter rest stage. Within seconds, the painful area settles and the body returns toward normal. This is the natural cycle from entering to releasing an asana.
When starting asana practice, pain in the first week is of a different kind. Just as beginners get pain when they first walk long distances, run, or start new exercises, early adaptation pain also appears in asanas. Such pain often comes before reaching full asana-hold, so it should not be confused with deeper functional pain. As practice improves, it takes longer for strong intolerable pain to come, and therefore hold time increases. The more this hold time grows (safely), the more benefit comes.
After some weeks or months, if pain no longer increases in a posture, you may need to move into a fuller version of that asana. For example, in Paschimottanasana, first you may only hold the feet with difficulty. Later, when that becomes easy, bend further toward knees. Later still, bring head to knees; later, chest toward thighs. Progress step by step. If a stage becomes too easy and no meaningful sensation appears, go deeper safely or move to another useful posture. If no sensation at all appears in a posture, internal change may be minimal.
But do not force the body stubbornly just because "more pain is better." Forcing can cause injury or internal strain. Advance gradually, with awareness and control.
Many people do asanas in a way that avoids pain completely. Then benefit becomes very low. If no sensation appears at all, either the posture is not done correctly, or required tension/compression is missing. Some people do 30 or 40 postures daily but do not hold any posture to meaningful depth, so results remain weak. Others keep moving in posture like exercise (forward/back/side counts), and then blood and energy do not focus in the target area. That gives exercise-type effect, not asana effect.
Some repeat one asana 3 or 4 times rapidly, entering and exiting before deep hold starts. This round-style repetition usually does not give full asana result. One correct, complete hold is often enough. If repeating is needed, give full rest first, then do the posture again properly, and rest again. Without this, rounds behave like exercise and the core asana result may be near zero.
Today many people are used to low-effort, comfort-based living. If we try to keep asanas fully comfortable with no sensation, benefit will be poor. Pain from over-exercise can be harmful, but controlled sensation during asana-hold can be beneficial. Observe each posture carefully. If no sensation appears, review your method. Sometimes not pain but tingling, pressure, heaviness, or another distinct sensation may appear - that also indicates working effect. The number of asanas done daily is less important than the quality and depth of each hold.
Note: Vajrasana, Padmasana, Sukhasana, Gomukhasana and similar seated postures are often used for meditation, prayer, longer comfortable sitting, and concentration. Do not judge these by pain. You may remain in them for as long as needed. These postures often benefit the mind more than the body, so absence of physical pain does not mean absence of benefit.

8. How to Stay in Asana Posture?

  • In asana-hold, do not move - do not let random thoughts run
  • Keep mind on the posture - close the eyes and observe
  • Do asanas with attention - place mind where sensation appears
  • Observe changes with closed eyes - open eyes bring distraction
From the moment you enter an asana until you release it, staying still in that posture is called asana-hold state. Most of the physical benefit depends on this holding time. The quality of benefit also depends on your thoughts and mental condition during this stage. If the mind is calm and free from random thinking, full benefit comes. If mind runs in many directions, benefit drops greatly. So let us understand how to stay in this stage for full result.
While practicing, many people keep watching others - how they bend, how long they stay, and whether they are doing better or not. Some keep eyes open and keep looking around. Some listen to songs or talk while practicing at home. Some keep chatting with others during hold. If there is no talking or looking around, some say they feel bored. But if mind is placed elsewhere and eyes keep wandering, not even half result comes.
The secret of asanas is to stop the mind from running. Correct asana practice brings a state where the mind stays steadily within the body for a long time, even after practice. If this does not happen, the asana-hold quality was not complete. To experience this state, we must know and follow our role during asana-hold.
1. Keep eyes closed: As soon as you enter the asana, close your eyes fully. In every posture, keep them closed throughout hold stage. When releasing, briefly manage transition safely, and again close the eyes in rest stage. With practice, you can stay with closed eyes through hold and release transitions too.
For the mind to turn inward, eyes must close. Asana practice is a disciplined, almost sacred activity. Such practice needs concentration and sincerity. Closed eyes improve concentration and reduce new distractions. With closed eyes, we notice our own mistakes. With open eyes, we mostly notice others' mistakes. When eyes close, talking also naturally reduces. If eyes and mouth are quiet, inner changes become clear. So during asana practice, these two rules are essential: keep eyes closed, avoid unnecessary speech.
2. Keep the mind steady: When mind stays on what we are doing, that is meditation. When body is doing one thing and mind is elsewhere, that is distraction. Asana practice is body-meditation. If this body-meditation becomes stable, mental meditation also becomes easier. With meditative asana practice, we gain more energy, deeper benefit, and mental transformation.
As soon as you enter hold stage, close eyes and place the mind on the area where pain or clear sensation is felt most. Changes are happening there because of the posture. By observing that area, you understand what is happening inside. This prevents other thoughts and gives calmness and strength. Focused attention also helps direct more functional energy to that area. It also helps you know when to release the posture.
The ability to stay longer in hold depends on mind stability. With daily observation and gradual increase in hold time, results improve faster. Practice becomes smoother and less boring, even for an hour or more. Over time, you begin to wait eagerly for asana time. With meditative practice, skipping asanas feels like losing something valuable that day.
Then you understand that asanas are not only for physical fitness. Without this method, people may think asanas and exercise are the same. If that feeling remains, asana practice is not yet understood deeply. The full secret of asanas is mindful practice.
Some may doubt: even if I try to focus, will the mind really stay steady? In hold stage, body changes immediately send signals through nerves to the brain, so mind has natural input to observe. If eyes open, new distractions enter and mind runs again. With eyes closed, mind can stay with internal signals.
Even if distraction comes in between, gently bring the mind back to the asana-hold. I request everyone to practice each posture this way - with closed eyes and inner awareness - to receive the full physical and mental benefits of asanas.

9. Why Should We Rest Between Two Asanas?

  • If you do postures nonstop, fatigue comes - so remember to rest in between
  • After releasing a posture, give rest - then circulation completes properly
  • After release, deep breaths begin - with rest, mental change appears
  • If rest is given after each posture - mind can enter a meditative state
Many people wonder: is rest really needed between asanas? Some skip rest and quickly move from one posture to the next, thinking that more speed, more sweat, and more number of asanas means better practice. In one hour they may do 30 or 40 postures and feel proud of the number. But this approach is not useful if proper rest is missing.
Doing many asanas without rest reduces results. To get full benefit, we must understand why rest is needed and how much rest to give. Let us look at the reasons.
First reason: During a posture, where pressure builds and discomfort appears, circulation in that area may reduce or shift. Blood that cannot enter that area accumulates around nearby regions. After release, that stored blood rushes into the previously compressed area. This rebound flow is a key benefit of asanas.
For this rebound effect to complete properly, rest is necessary after release. If you move immediately into the next posture, muscles and nerves tighten again and circulation does not complete. Then benefit remains partial. Without rest, results often stay around 50 to 60 percent. This is the first major reason for rest.
Second reason: During asana-hold, breathing may be restricted to some degree. In some postures breathing becomes shallow, muscles work hard, and mild fatigue or strain appears. In others, airflow is reduced more and energy use rises. So we release only after these effects appear.
After release, the body needs time to recover from fatigue and breath strain. Breathing rate should come down, deep breaths should happen, and the body should return toward normal. If we start the next asana before this recovery, we cannot hold it properly, and hold time drops in all postures. So rest is needed until natural recovery occurs.
Third reason: Right after releasing each posture, deep breathing starts naturally. With these deeper breaths, more oxygen enters the body, and the mind settles. Thoughts reduce and mental calmness increases. This calm state is one of the greatest gifts of asana practice.
Most people spend the whole day in restless thinking. Asanas give at least one period of daily peace - but this peace is felt mainly during the rest interval after each posture. If you keep moving and skip this interval, mental benefit does not develop fully. Then practice may feel tiring instead of refreshing. The comfort and inner ease expected from each asana are received mainly in the rest stage.
Keeping these three reasons in mind, decide rest time between postures properly. One fixed rest time is not right for all asanas. After each release, rest until fatigue, breath strain, and pain settle, and breathing returns closer to normal. This is also when deep mental calmness can be experienced.
As a guideline, do not keep rest below one minute after each posture. Depending on need, give 2 to 3 minutes, and for some postures even more may be necessary. If time permits, longer rest can help. With proper rest, asana practice itself becomes like meditation. So do not ask only, "How many asanas did I do?" Ask instead, "How peacefully did I do them?" Not "How much did I sweat?" but "How well did my body relax?"

10. What to Do in the Gap Between Two Asanas?

  • Close your eyes in the gap - then you will know its value
  • Keep attention on breath - observe breathing with the mind
  • Take rest between asanas - then receive full benefit of practice
The gap between two asanas is called the rest state. Many people make mistakes here. As soon as they release a posture, they look around, talk to others, ask doubts, chat, or listen to songs. If we do this immediately after release, neither body nor mind relaxes properly. When full rest is missing, asana benefit decreases. So let us see what should be done after releasing each posture.
1. Keep eyes closed: As soon as a posture is completed, keep eyes closed. If eyes open, new visual input brings new thoughts. To get complete rest, eyes should remain closed for the full rest interval.
2. Take a proper resting posture: During asana-hold, many body parts stay in tension. So right after release, sit or lie in a rest posture where the whole body from toes to head is free from tightness. If the asana was done sitting, sit relaxed for rest; if lying, rest lying down. Let all body parts become loose and unsupported. This improves circulation and allows nerves to recover energy. This step is very important between asanas.
3. Keep the mind on breath: After taking a rest posture and releasing body tension, place the mind on breathing. Observe breath flow near the area below the nostrils and above the upper lip. Fixing attention there reduces new and old thoughts. If thoughts keep running, body and mind do not recover fully. To get stronger asana results, this rest interval should be used to settle both mind and body under conscious control.
4. Experience full calmness: If a posture is done correctly for proper time, deep breaths begin immediately after release. More oxygen enters and supports body needs. Within two or three deep breaths, the mind becomes very stable and quiet. In this gap between asanas, we can directly experience a thought-light state of mind.
This calm state often comes naturally after release, if the first three steps are followed. Many people stop asana practice because they miss this inner peace and therefore do not understand its value. If we receive such calmness without struggle and still do not allow ourselves time to experience it, that is a loss. A thought-free mind, even for a short period, gives strong energy and strength to the body. This is one key difference between asanas and ordinary exercise.
A mind that becomes clear and peaceful immediately after an asana is close to meditation. Meditation brings better capacity, clearer thinking, joy, and health. Even if one cannot do other meditation forms, this meditative quality can be developed through proper asana-rest practice. Asanas are that valuable. By following these four steps in every gap between postures, everyone can receive full daily benefit from asana practice.

11. When and Where Should Asanas Be Done?

  • Practice before sunrise - later hours have less calm vibration
  • Do asanas on an empty stomach - open-air practice feels best
Just as time and place matter for any good work, they also matter for asana practice. Proper environment and suitable location are important. If asanas are done randomly, full benefit may not come, and in some cases problems may arise. So let us see when and where practice is best.
When to practice: Asanas do not help only the body; they also bring mental changes. Through asanas, mind learns to stay where the body is. To receive this mental benefit fully, early morning is best. If the surrounding environment is not supportive, physical benefit may still come, but mental benefit may reduce.
Before sunrise, the atmosphere is quieter, cleaner, and mentally supportive. This time is often considered rich in prana shakti (vital energy) and positive vibration. After people wake up, restlessness and stress increase in the environment. So early hours are preferred. Especially brahma muhurta - roughly from 4:00 AM to 6:00 AM - is considered excellent for asana practice. Waking at that time also supports disciplined living. Disturbances at home are usually fewer then, helping the mind stay inward from start to finish.
If waking at that time is not possible, practice can still be done before 7:00-8:00 AM. But expect more disturbances such as phone calls, conversations, or visitors, which reduce quality. Do not take bed coffee or similar drinks before practice. Asanas should be done on an empty stomach.
If some key asanas are practiced in the evening, keep stomach empty then as well. A good evening window is around 5:00 PM to 6:30 PM. Still, as far as possible, morning practice is best for carrying calmness through the day.
Where to practice: Early morning open-air practice is ideal. Even in winter or mild mist, it can be done; pranayama first helps warm the body, and asanas also generate body heat. If living in crowded areas, terrace practice is good. If terrace is not possible, a veranda can be used. If that is also not possible, practice in a bedroom with doors and windows well open.
Avoid fans during practice. Avoid places near kitchen or storage areas. Ensure stale exhaled air can move out and fresh clean air can enter. Sit where natural airflow is available.
Daily asana practice in a peaceful morning environment, with minimal disturbance and good fresh air, supports a positive and balanced whole day. If possible, adding a small evening session is also very beneficial.

12. How to Prepare for Asanas?

  • Drink the first round of water after waking - prepare for first bowel movement
  • Do pranayama first - only then begin asana practice
  • If pranayama is done first - asana results improve greatly
1. First round of water: After waking, pass urine first if needed. Some people also pass stool immediately along with urine - that is good. If not, rinse your mouth and then drink water. Those with cold, cough, breathlessness, phlegm, or acidity may use lukewarm water; others can use normal water. Do not drink refrigerated water.
Drink in one or two rounds, about 1 to 1.5 liters within 5 to 6 minutes. As much as your stomach can comfortably hold is generally better. Drinking a larger amount at once creates abdominal pressure, which can help smooth bowel movement. If water is sipped too slowly in very small amounts, pressure may not build enough and bowel movement may not start easily.
2. First bowel movement: After drinking water as above, abdominal nerves begin to respond, and bowel movement starts (gastrocolic reflex). Water pressure in the stomach and lower abdomen pushes the stool forward. After drinking, do not start other tasks immediately. For 5 to 10 minutes, walk gently inside the house and keep awareness on bowel activity.
This awareness helps relaxation of bowel-holding nerves and supports smooth movement. Go to the toilet when a clear natural urge becomes strong. If you go at the right urge, bowel emptying is often quick and smooth. Make this a daily routine. Then complete brushing/cleaning routines. Wear clothing that keeps the waist loose and comfortable for asanas and other physical work. In about 20 to 25 minutes after water intake, physical activity or asana practice can begin.
3. What to do before starting asanas: After 20 to 25 minutes from water intake, you may begin. Ideally, do pranayama before asanas. About 25 to 30 minutes may be used for it. Doing pranayama first prepares breathing and body, and helps you hold asanas longer and better.
Those who already practice Surya Namaskara and wish to do a few rounds (for example 4 or 5) may do them first, then pranayama, then asanas. This sequence prepares the system well and improves results. If someone chooses only asanas, they can start after basic morning routines. Still, pranayama and asanas are strongly connected and give best result together. Doing both brings fuller benefit.
4. Things needed: 1. Keep a hand towel nearby. It helps if there is mucus movement or sweating during practice.
2. Use a soft yoga carpet or one or two folded soft blankets placed to support full body length. A soft base helps longer and more comfortable holding. Very spongy surfaces are not ideal because stable posture becomes difficult. A very thin blanket can bring pain early. Keep the base comfortably soft but stable.
5. What to do after asanas: About 5 to 10 minutes after finishing, you may drink another 1 to 1.5 liters of water and again keep awareness on bowel movement. This may support a smooth second bowel movement because asana practice stimulates intestinal movement.
After that, a cool-water head wash may help. Pranayama and asanas generate heat in body and head, and cooling the head can support comfort and circulation. After bath, start natural food (prakriti-siddha diet) appropriately.
Waking early morning, drinking enough water, ensuring smooth bowel movement, and then doing pranayama before asanas is an excellent all-round method to prepare the body.

13. Description of Asanas

Many people feel afraid when they hear the word asanas. They see twists, bends, and different postures and think, "How can we do all this?" So they get discouraged. There are hundreds of asanas, each designed for specific benefits and higher attainments. But because many people do not know their full practical value, they stay away.
To help people first understand the health value of asanas, this chapter explains a selected set of important postures. These are practical, easier for most people, and useful for common modern health problems.
Keeping modern lifestyle and limited time in mind, I am explaining 15 key asanas that can give strong results. These 15 postures affect the body from feet to head and improve the health of muscles, nerves, bones, cells, and blood vessels by influencing all major systems.
Even if one cannot do many other asanas, these 15 are designed to support complete health in a practical way. Most people can complete them in about 45 to 60 minutes daily. Practice these first for a few months, and then, if interest and time remain, add other asanas.
The postures are given in a daily practice order. Read this chapter two or three times carefully first. Understand what changes each asana creates in which parts of the body. Then begin practice with clear understanding. If practiced daily without break, these asanas can give excellent physical and mental results. Make life healthier and happier.

Vajrasana

This posture is called Vajrasana because it is considered very valuable - like a diamond - among asanas. It gives high benefit with very little strain and in less time.
Method: In Vajrasana, the front bony part from knee to foot rests on the floor. Since this area has less cushioning, hard floor can cause pain and make long sitting difficult. Prepare a soft surface so you can sit comfortably and still for 20 to 30 minutes. Use a soft yoga carpet, folded cloth, or soft blanket.
1. Keep both knees close together and kneel. Stretch both feet backward and place a thin folded cloth under the ankle joints.
2. Keep both big toes near each other, with a small gap between heels, and sit down on the legs. Each buttock rests over each heel.
3. Place hands on the thighs and keep the trunk upright at about 90 degrees. Stay like a pillar without bending as long as you hold Vajrasana.
Important points: 1. Why keep cloth under ankles? Around 60 to 70 percent of body weight may fall on this area in Vajrasana. Since these joints are not usually stretched like this, pain can come in 2 to 3 minutes for some people, and in 5 to 6 minutes for others. A thin folded cloth reduces pressure and allows longer steady sitting with less discomfort.
2. Beginners cannot sit long in Vajrasana immediately. Pain and tingling may come in 5 to 10 minutes. Increase duration slowly and gradually as the body adapts.
Asana-hold stage: Keep hands relaxed, spine straight, eyes fully closed, and mind on breath. Sit calmly without random thought. This posture usually does not create breathlessness or heavy fatigue. With upright spine, nerve flow between brain and body becomes smooth. Lungs stay expanded, so deeper breathing happens naturally. More oxygen reaches body and mind, and mental calmness increases.
As long as legs tolerate it, continue the hold. Because knees are deeply folded and weight falls there, blood flow toward feet may reduce during hold; tingling in feet and pain near ankles can appear. With gradual practice, one can sit 20 to 30 minutes comfortably. During the hold, keeping mind on breath without distraction is very important. When you can no longer continue, slowly stretch the legs and move to rest posture.
Rest stage: Stretch both legs fully and let them relax out to the sides. Place hands behind and gently lean the neck back so the whole body becomes free.
In this rest stage, keep attention on breath and try to remain calm. Soon after stretching legs, tingling and pulling sensations can increase for 1 to 2 minutes, then gradually reduce. During Vajrasana, feet may look pale due to reduced flow; after release, blood rushes quickly toward feet, and they may turn reddish. This rebound circulation is an important benefit.
While holding Vajrasana, upper body areas get strong benefit; during rest after release, areas from knees down to toes get strong benefit. To receive this fully, stay in rest stage with legs stretched for about 4 to 5 minutes. Full rest is essential.
Wrong posture: Vajrasana is correct only when trunk stays upright around 90 degrees. Some bend the back forward and drop the neck. This is incorrect.
Do not sit as shown in the wrong example. If trunk bends forward, diaphragm position changes, lungs cannot expand well, oxygen intake drops, and breathing rate rises. Nerve-brain coordination is also affected, and mind will not stay calm. So benefit of Vajrasana is lost. Always keep the trunk upright. If back pain comes, release the posture - do not bend forward inside the posture.
Benefits
1. Vajrasana supports pranayama well. Upright trunk and expanded chest help better inhalation and exhalation, so pranayama can be more effective.
2. It gives strong mental calmness for meditation. Mind stabilizes quickly in this posture, and entering meditation becomes easier. Upright spine is very important for meditation.
3. Most asanas are not done after meals, but Vajrasana is an exception and is often recommended after food. It supports digestion, reduces post-meal heaviness, and helps gas release.
4. It can be used while praying, in temples, during devotional activities, after meals, or in free time. It helps mental focus in such activities.
5. If mind is disturbed, irritated, overthinking, or sleep is poor, sitting in Vajrasana with breath awareness for 5 to 10 minutes can give relief.
6. It improves leg circulation strongly and supports foot health. Tingling, burning, prickling, and ankle discomfort can reduce with practice.
7. For those without knee pain, daily practice keeps knee joints mobile and can help prevent future stiffness.
8. Appetite can improve. For indigestion, practicing Vajrasana twice daily may help.
9. For bloating, trapped gas, and abdominal heaviness, sitting in Vajrasana for about 10 minutes may give quick relief.
10. People with mild back pain may find floor sitting difficult in ordinary crossed-leg posture. In such situations, Vajrasana can be a better sitting option.
Note
1. No need to add Vajrasana separately as a long extra session every time. It can be used during pranayama, meditation, free time, or after meals. This saves time and gives extra benefit.
2. Those with severe knee pain should avoid Vajrasana.
3. Entering and releasing quickly gives little benefit. Try to hold at least 5 to 10 minutes, or longer as tolerated.

Matsyasana

This asana is traditionally associated with Sage Matsyendra. It is said that one who masters this posture can float on water like a fish, so it is called Matsyasana.
Method: Before starting, sit near the front side of the mat/blanket.
1. Those who can fold legs well may sit in Padmasana first. But if Padmasana is difficult, do not think you are unfit for this asana. You may sit in Sukhasana with legs close and practice.
2. Place one forearm/elbow on the floor and start bending the chest backward.
3. Then place the second arm similarly on the other side.
4. Move both hands toward the legs and bend back so the upper back approaches the floor.
5. Place both palms under the thighs, press both elbows into the floor, lift the chest up from the waist, arch backward, and bend the head back so the crown touches the floor. The photo also shows the Sukhasana variation for those who cannot do Padmasana.
6. Keep the eyes fully closed.
Asana-hold stage: After reaching full posture as shown, keep thoughts quiet and place attention on the neck and throat region. Stay still as long as possible. Release when pain becomes too strong. To release, first bring head and chest down, free the hands, then stretch legs (or keep leg-fold if in Padmasana as needed). Then rise slowly and prepare for the next forward-bend posture.
Second variation: This asana can also be done in another way, which is easier for those who cannot fold legs well. Use whichever version is suitable. Those comfortable with Padmasana may continue that version.
Method
1. Lie on your back with head on the blanket. Keep both palms on floor, move them under the buttock area, and keep legs stretched.
2. Keep legs and palms as they are, then lift chest and head upward using elbow support.
3. Then arch chest backward and place the crown on the floor. In this position, waist/back do not rest fully on the floor; weight is mainly through head and elbows.
4. Keep eyes fully closed and stay as long as comfortably possible.
Wrong posture: In this asana, maximum benefit comes to neck and throat only when the neck is properly arched and the crown touches the floor. If this key point is missed, overall benefit drops even if the rest looks fine. See the examples of wrong alignment below.
The chest is lifted, but the crown is not on the floor; instead the back of the neck touches. So neck extension is insufficient.
Here the hands are not correctly placed under the chest area, so the waist does not lift properly. Then the crown cannot reach floor correctly, and the neck position is wrong. If waist and neck do not arch properly, benefit is minimal. Learn these corrections carefully.
Benefits
1. Very useful for thyroid imbalance. During hold, the thyroid region is compressed and stretched; after release, circulation and oxygen supply improve strongly, supporting gland function.
2. It also influences the parathyroid glands behind the thyroid. Since these glands regulate calcium balance (important for muscles and heart contraction-relaxation), better circulation here can support bone strength and flexibility.
3. In thyroid swelling linked with iodine deficiency, daily Matsyasana along with proper iodine intake (diet or medical guidance) may help reduce swelling faster.
4. Thyroid issues are common now, even in children. As a preventive measure, regular practice can help maintain healthier gland function.
5. Helpful for neck pain (including cervical spondylitis patterns), common in office and business workers who keep neck bent forward for long hours. Backward neck extension in Matsyasana offers relief. If neck pain is combined with back pain, the lying variation is safer. Those with neck pain may practice twice daily; if needed, repeat once after rest.
6. Good preventive practice for people without current neck pain as well. It helps avoid future neck stiffness from daily habits such as driving or long forward bending activities.
7. Neck muscles and blood vessels become healthier, helping better blood supply toward the head.
8. For some people with back pain, controlled backward bending in this posture may provide relief.
9. It can support the tonsil region and improve local resistance, helping reduce repeated throat infections.
10. The chest opens strongly in this posture. Rib cage, intercostal muscles, and lungs get better expansion-contraction, so deeper breathing improves after release and can continue through the day.
Notes
1. Since neck and back are bent backward strongly here, it is useful to do a forward-bending asana afterward to rebalance.
2. But for people with active neck or back pain, avoid immediate forward bending after this posture.
3. Beginners should not hold beyond about 10-15 seconds initially. If done too long in the first 5-6 days, neck stiffness/pain may appear. After that adaptation phase, gradually increase hold time.
4. If neck pain appears, gentle local care such as warm compress (and suitable topical oil if preferred) may provide relief.
5. Some people do Matsyasana after Paschimottanasana and report good benefit. That is also acceptable. In general, starting with easier postures and then moving to harder ones gives better overall results. Here, Matsyasana and Yoga Mudrasana are kept earlier as lighter sequence choices.
6. In this sequence, Yoga Mudrasana is done immediately after Matsyasana, and rest is taken after that.

Yoga Mudrasana

This is one of the mudras in Hatha Yoga. Traditionally it is said to awaken kundalini and integrate mental energies, so it is called Yoga Mudra. Even if we are not practicing for that higher purpose, we can still gain many practical health benefits from it.
Method: Usually this is done after Matsyasana while remaining in Padmasana without opening the legs. If pain is high, you may release Padmasana after Matsyasana and re-enter for this asana. Keeping Padmasana continuously for both postures is preferred when possible. Staying in Padmasana for around 4 to 5 minutes in total creates temporary reduction in leg circulation; after release, strong rebound circulation reaches the feet.
1. Sit in the middle of the mat in Padmasana. Take both hands behind the back, make a fist with one hand, hold it with the other hand, keep arms close to chest, and bend the head so the chin moves toward chest. If Padmasana is not possible, do this in Sukhasana.
2. Close the eyes fully, bend forward from the waist, and exhale fully through both nostrils while lowering the head toward the floor. By the time the head reaches down, exhalation should be complete. Stay steady with crown toward floor and chin close to chest.
Asana-hold stage: After reaching full posture (crown down), hold the breath out (external retention) as long as comfortably possible. Keep attention on the throat region during this hold. When breath need rises, slowly lift the head and chest while inhaling gradually.
Rest stage: Release Padmasana, stretch both legs fully, place hands back, relax the neck backward, close eyes, and keep mind on breath flow (felt below nostrils/above upper lip). Rest for about one minute.
Other methods: If the above method is difficult, use the alternatives below. For overweight practitioners, the first alternative is often easier.
1. Sit in Sukhasana (simple cross-leg seat), raise both hands upward, then exhale while lowering hands and head toward floor. Keep chin close to chest. Hold breath out while in the posture, and inhale only while lifting back up. In some people hands may touch floor but head may not - that is fine. Benefit appears where effort is felt.
2. This can also be done from Vajrasana. If that feels easier, use that variation with the same holding principles.
Wrong posture: While bending forward, buttocks should remain grounded. If buttocks lift from floor, abdominal and intestinal compression benefit reduces. So bend waist and head without lifting the seat, unlike the wrong examples shown.
Benefits
1. Doing this immediately after Matsyasana gives alternating expansion and compression to thyroid and parathyroid regions. This improves blood flow and oxygenation in those glands.
2. External breath retention in this posture helps lungs gain stronger contraction capacity. After release, deeper inhalation becomes easier, training longer breathing patterns that improve daily energy.
3. Forward compression in this posture applies pressure to abdominal muscles and intestines, helping reduce abdominal fat gradually and improving bowel strength.
4. Along with Matsyasana, this posture can support improved thyroid function and may reduce dependency burden (with proper medical supervision).
5. For children with poor growth patterns, the Matsyasana + Yoga Mudrasana combination may support endocrine balance and growth support.
Notes
1. One round is enough. Do not repeat unnecessarily just because it feels easy.
2. Breath retention is used here specifically to create focused compression in thyroid and throat region. This retention method is specific to this asana in this sequence.
3. If Matsyasana is skipped, this asana may also be skipped in this specific paired protocol, because this posture is intended as the immediate forward counter to Matsyasana.
4. Many people do not feel strong discomfort in this posture, so with breath retention they usually release in about 20-25 seconds. That duration is generally enough for effect.
5. Those with neck pain, back pain, leg nerve issues, or similar problems should avoid this posture.

Vakrasana

This posture is called Vakrasana because the spine is twisted sideways in a curved manner.
Method: Vakrasana must be done on both sides. If you twist fully to one side, you must also twist to the other. Let us first do the left-side twist.
To the left side
1. Sit in the middle of the mat with both legs stretched straight and both hands behind on the floor. Keep the right leg straight and bend the left leg, bringing the left foot near the knee.
2. Stretch the right arm and place it over the upper side of the left knee. Keep the left leg firm and stable without wobbling.
3. Press against the left knee with the right arm and twist the waist and neck fully to the left. Right and left shoulders should come into one line. Some people may hear mild rib/vertebral clicks while twisting. Keep eyes fully closed.
Asana-hold stage: Twist as far left as possible and place calm attention on the waist area where sensation appears. Stay motionless with hands and legs fixed, like meditation. Longer steady hold gives better benefit. Release when pain in waist/side abdomen becomes too strong. Because the abdomen is twisted, breathing may stay more in upper chest and mild breath strain can be felt, especially in people with larger abdomen. Stay only within your capacity; do not force beyond strength.
Rest stage: To release, keep eyes closed, bring neck back to center, slowly release the right arm, place both hands back, and sit freely in Sukhasana with neck relaxed backward. Breathing now becomes deeper and slower. Keep attention on breath and avoid new thoughts.
Immediately after release, you may feel blood rushing to waist and side-abdomen regions. Where pain was felt during hold, increased circulation now reaches that area. To get full benefit, remain calmly in this rest posture for about 1 to 2 minutes. After breathing returns naturally, do Vakrasana on the right side.
To the right side: Now keep left leg straight, bend right leg and bring right foot near left knee. Stretch left arm over right knee and keep right leg steady. Press with left arm and twist waist/neck fully to the right. Keep eyes closed and hold with the same awareness as on left side. After release, rest again in Sukhasana for 1 to 2 minutes.
Full stage: After good practice, you may place the bent foot closer toward the buttock. This gives deeper compression to abdominal and waist muscles, and breathing reduces more. So longer hold becomes harder. Move into this gradually with practice. Beginners should stay with the basic method.
Another method: This variation is for people with higher body weight, larger thighs, higher abdomen, or rounder body shape. Lean people may need full twist for deep effect; in heavier bodies, even partial twist can give similar effect because abdominal and waist muscles compress earlier. So do not feel discouraged.
Keep left leg straight without collapsing. Bend right leg and place right foot somewhat away (near calf area instead of very near knee) if needed for comfort. Place left forearm/hand against right knee and press gently while turning neck and waist as far as possible. Shoulders may not come into one line in heavier bodies; that is acceptable. Position hand and leg where it is practical and stable. Stay as long as possible without breath distress. Then release slowly and take longer rest in Sukhasana if needed.
Wrong posture: Many people make errors in Vakrasana. Then spine does not rotate fully, and abdominal/waist muscles do not tighten effectively. If no meaningful tightness, side sensation, or slight breath challenge appears even after holding, posture is likely incorrect.
1. In this wrong example, the left leg slipped sideways. Because the pushing arm is not properly placed, waist rotated only halfway. Shoulders are not aligned, and hand position is off-line. This can feel comfortable but gives poor effect. True rotation should create proper muscular and breathing response.
2. Here the left leg has collapsed and body has tilted onto one buttock while the other lifts. When body tilts, waist/abdomen do not compress correctly, and circulation effects are reduced. Keep the trunk upright in one line while twisting to avoid this mistake.
Benefits
1. Helps reduce side waist fat folds, especially around the flanks.
2. Deep twisting and holding can help reduce localized fat in areas where strongest side compression is felt, helping the waist become slimmer.
3. Improves mobility between spinal segments and surrounding muscles. Helps reduce stiffness during daily forward-bending and turning movements.
4. Enhances circulation to muscles on both sides of spine. Rotational work from neck to lower spine supports vertebral and disc health.
5. Even for those without excess fat, this posture tones and tightens the waist region.
6. Turning the neck to both sides helps reduce cervical stiffness and supports freer neck movement.
Notes
1. In the first 5 to 6 days, hold only about 10 to 15 seconds. After adaptation, increase hold gradually.
2. After a few months, if this posture causes no meaningful sensation even with proper hold, it may be reduced and replaced by Ardha Matsyendrasana.
3. Those with good flexibility may skip Vakrasana from the beginning and do Ardha Matsyendrasana instead, since it provides similar benefits with greater depth.

Ardha Matsyendrasana

This posture was taught by the great yogi Matsyendra, so it is called Ardha Matsyendrasana. In nature, some animals move their spine forward, backward, and sideways in many ways as soon as they wake up, to remove laziness and loosen the body. This natural movement of the spine helps energy flow through the body. In the same way, this posture creates many useful movements in our body and releases energy in the morning.
Method: In Ardha Matsyendrasana also, we practice separately on both sides, similar to Vakrasana. First, let us learn the right side.
To the right side
1. Sit in the middle of a mat, stretch both legs fully, and place both hands on the floor behind the back. Then bend the left leg and place the left heel between the buttocks, sitting on the left foot. Next, bend and lift the right leg, cross it over the left thigh, and place the right foot on the floor.
2. Now bring the left hand across the right knee (as in Vakrasana) and hold the right kneecap area.
3. Press the right leg inward toward the left armpit, turn the waist and neck to the right, lift the right hand from the floor and place it on the waist, and balance the body on the left leg without moving. Keep the eyes fully closed.
Posture Position: After reaching the final pose, keep your attention on the lower abdomen and sides of the waist, and remain steady without falling or moving for as long as possible. If placing the right hand on the waist makes you lose balance, keep the hand on the floor in between for support until you gain practice. To release the pose, first bring the back hand forward, turn the neck forward, and then slowly release the rest of the posture.
Resting Position: Immediately after releasing Ardha Matsyendrasana, keep the eyes closed, stretch both legs fully, keep them relaxed, place the hands on the floor behind the body, let the neck relax backward, and sit calmly in Sukhasana for 1 to 2 minutes with attention on the breath. Stay in rest until tiredness reduces fully and breathing returns to normal. Then do the posture on the second side.
To the left side
1. Sit in the middle of a mat, stretch both legs fully, and place the hands on the floor behind the back. Then bend the right leg and place the heel between the buttocks, sitting on the right foot. Next, bend and lift the left leg, cross it over the right thigh, and place it on the floor on that side.
2. Now bring the right hand across the left knee and hold the left kneecap area.
3. Press the left leg inward toward the right armpit with the right hand, turn the waist and neck fully to the left, lift the left hand from the floor and place it on the waist, and balance the body on the right leg without moving. Keep the eyes fully closed. Stay in the posture as above for as long as possible, then take full rest in the resting pose for 1 to 2 minutes.
Another Method: People with heavy thighs or a large abdomen may find this posture difficult at first. If there is no major discomfort, they can try with small modifications as follows.
Method: Bend the left leg but keep it beside the buttock instead of under it. Bend the right leg and place it near the left knee area. Press the right leg with the left hand. If holding the left knee area is difficult, hold as far as possible. Keep pressing the right leg with the left hand, and turn the waist and neck fully to the side. The right hand may stay on the floor for support or be placed on the waist. Even with small changes in hand and leg position, good benefit comes if the waist and neck are twisted well. Practice on both sides and stay as long as possible.
Wrong Position: Sometimes hand and leg placement looks correct, but the body is too relaxed, showing that the waist is not really twisting. To rotate the waist properly, press the leg firmly with the hand and twist. If you actively press and twist, everyone gets results. If this posture is not done correctly, breathing remains normal. But if the waist is twisted deeply, breathing becomes heavier than in Vakrasana, and that indicates real work in the pose. If there is no strain anywhere and the posture feels too easy and too comfortable, that usually means something is wrong. In that case, reset and twist properly again. Do not just do the pose casually and move on.
Benefits
1. This posture is about twice as difficult as Vakrasana. Because both legs are folded very close, it especially reduces fat around the hip area. This effect is less in Vakrasana. Many people store more fat in the hip region and have lower blood flow there. During the posture, pain may be felt in that area. While holding the pose, circulation there is reduced, and after release, increased circulation supports healing in that part.
2. Folding both legs close and twisting to the side gives a strong massage-like effect to the abdomen and intestines, which is beneficial.
3. In this posture, deep side twisting of the legs and waist gives strong pressure to the pancreas gland (pancreas) that produces insulin. This improves its contraction and expansion, which can improve function and hormone production. This posture is very useful for people with diabetes. Even those without diabetes can practice it as prevention.
4. Due to the movements created in organs below the navel, this posture can strengthen that region and may help protect against future problems like hernia, hydrocele, and prostate gland disorders.
5. This posture reduces fat on both sides of the waist and helps make the waist slimmer. It is one of the best postures for shaping the sides of the waist.
6. Nerves inside and around the spine are strongly stimulated in this posture and gain more strength.
7. In daily life, we rarely twist the waist to the sides and back. So the vertebrae and surrounding muscles become tight. In this posture, because the waist is twisted deeply to both sides, the vertebrae and supporting muscles become freer, helping you bend and turn through the day without discomfort.
8. Daily practice of Ardha Matsyendrasana helps keep spinal discs (the sponge-like pads between vertebrae) in proper position and protects the spine and nerves from pressure. Because of this, waist-related problems may reduce even as age increases.
Note
1. People with low back pain or sciatica-like leg nerve pain should not do this posture.
2. While learning, it is better to practice Vakrasana well for 10 to 20 days first, and then do this posture. Those who are very overweight or have more difficulty should progress gradually.
3. A common mistake is turning to the wrong side. Then there is no result, and the waist does not twist properly. To avoid mistakes: if the right leg is placed up, put the left hand over it. If the left leg is placed up, use the right hand and press the leg while twisting.
4. If this posture is not possible for people with heavy thighs or a large abdomen, they may skip it without worry. The same core benefits can be gained well from Vakrasana until this posture becomes possible.

Gomukhasana

In this posture, both knees are brought close, one above the other, and the shape looks like a cow's face. So this posture is called Gomukhasana.
Method: This posture is also done on both sides. One time with the right-side setup, and one time with the left-side setup. First, let us do the right side.
To the right side
1. Sit with both legs stretched straight. First bend the right leg close to the body and place the right foot near the outer side of the left buttock. Then bend the left leg, hold the left foot with both hands, and pull it over the right thigh toward the outer side of the right buttock. Pull both legs so both knees come close together.
2. Move the right hand behind the back as if you are rubbing the back, and push it upward as much as possible. Then raise the left hand straight up beside the ear with the palm facing upward, bend it back at the elbow, and bring it to the back. Try to clasp the fingers of both hands. Close the eyes. Keep the left elbow pointing upward in one line. Keep the neck and waist straight.
Posture Position: Try to pull both hands closer and maintain the shape properly, keeping the body steady. Keep attention on the right upper arm region and remain in the pose as long as possible. There may be strong squeezing pain near the right upper arm and shoulder. Stay only as long as you can bear comfortably. To release, first let go of the hands, lower the top hand slowly, then remove the bottom hand slowly and carefully. Then stretch the left leg forward, release the right leg gently, and stretch both legs.
Resting Position: Stretch both legs freely, place both palms on the floor, relax the neck backward, and rest in Sukhasana. Keep attention on the breath and stay calm for about one minute. During this rest phase, blood flow that was restricted in the limbs during the pose increases and reaches painful areas better. Without this rest, full benefit is not obtained. Stay in rest until arm pain, tingling, and leg numbness reduce and hands and legs return to normal. Take more rest time if needed.
To the left side: Now place the left leg below and the right leg above. Place the left hand from below behind the back, and place the right hand from above behind the back. Clasp both hands and follow the same rules as before. Release slowly and take full rest for 1 to 2 minutes.
Other Methods: People with heavy thighs or side fat may not be able to sit in the full leg position. They should not stop the posture completely. To get the hand benefits of Gomukhasana, they can sit in a suitable leg position and still practice. Those who cannot sit on the floor or have waist problems can sit upright on a stool and place the hands in the same way. In any sitting method, keeping the waist straight is the main point. If one sits in full Gomukhasana, both hand and leg benefits are good. In the following simpler options, hand benefits come well, but leg benefits are less. You can practice in three ways.
1. In Vajrasana: Bend both legs backward, sit on the feet, and hold the hands from above and below as described. In Vajrasana, alternate the hand position on each side.
2. In Ardha Gomukhasana: For most people, the lower leg bends well. But the top leg may bend only partly due to heavy thighs or stiff joints. Even if full Gomukhasana is not possible, those who can bend as shown in the photo should practice this way. Even partial practice gives better benefit than Vajrasana or Sukhasana. So those who cannot do full Gomukhasana should first try this version, and then use other methods if needed. Even in Ardha Gomukhasana, keep the waist straight.
3. In Sukhasana: Some people cannot do even Vajrasana or Ardha Gomukhasana. They may sit in Sukhasana and practice the hand position.
Some people have heavy upper arms, and some have stiff joints, so the hands do not meet easily. Even if they do not meet, push the hands as far as possible and hold there for benefit. To help the hands meet, use a handkerchief or cloth in the top hand, let it hang behind, and hold it with the lower hand, then slowly move the hands closer using that support. If needed, another trained person may gently help bring the hands closer. Do this carefully. If pushed without care, shoulder joints can slip. Even if the hands do not fully meet, if there is good pain while doing the posture, the benefit is still strong. Do not worry.
Wrong Position:
Even in this simple posture, many people make one or more mistakes and get less benefit. A common mistake is not keeping the top hand straight upward. Most people can raise it, but they stop midway. Push it more and keep it in a proper line. Also move the lower hand upward well. If the hands are just placed loosely behind, there is little result. If the waist bends forward, the hands do not get proper stretch, and the posture feels easy even after a long hold. In the photo, both neck and hand are bent forward, and the lower hand is not tightened while the waist bends forward. Compare this with a correctly done posture and the difference becomes clear. Any mistake like this reduces the benefit.
Benefits
1. This posture helps people with heavy upper arms and shoulders by reducing fat in those areas and improving shape. For such areas, there are few effective methods, so this is very useful when practiced twice daily.
2. As age increases, many people get shoulder-joint pain. This posture improves movement in the shoulder joint and supports natural lubrication, reducing pain. It can help prevent future shoulder pain.
3. Many people suffer from hand tingling, weak finger grip, and nerve pain in the arm. These often do not improve with medicines alone. They are linked to changes in nerves and circulation in the shoulder-arm region. In this posture, circulation to the lower arm is reduced while holding, then increased strongly after release up to the fingers. This can reduce tingling. Also, strongly linking and pulling the hands increases nerve and muscle mobility, making them freer and improving finger grip.
4. By linking the hands from above and below and tightening firmly, muscles of the lower arm, upper arm, and shoulder get a deep wringing action, like twisting a wet cloth. This removes stiffness and increases flexibility. Then both arms can move upward and backward more freely.
5. Daily practice improves hand and shoulder movement, helping in daily actions like cleaning the back, scratching the back comfortably, and wearing clothes more easily.
6. People with many kinds of health issues can usually practice this posture. Even during menstruation, women may do this while sitting in Sukhasana or Vajrasana.
7. Daily practice of the Gomukhasana leg position helps bring knee and hip joints under better control. In the future, it may help reduce risks like joint stiffness, arthritis, and joint degeneration, even with age.
8. If this sitting posture becomes easy, one can sit steadily in this pose for a long time during spiritual talks or temple visits and keep the mind calm. Unlike some poses, this does not require a very soft surface and can be practiced in many places. Even now, many elders sit in this posture.
9. If one sits in Gomukhasana with legs still for 10 to 15 minutes (without hand lock), blood flow increases to the feet after release and helps reduce burning sensation and tingling in the feet.
10. Sitting with both knees brought close in Gomukhasana helps reduce fat in the hip region and improves circulation there.
11. People with mental anxiety may feel calm through Gomukhasana. Soon after releasing the pose, deeper oxygen intake is often experienced.
Note
1. In this posture, some people can move one hand behind easily but not the other. In one side both hands may meet, but on the other side they may not. Beginners often worry about this, but it is normal. In daily life we use one hand more behind the back for bathing, dressing, or scratching, so that hand moves better and meets the top hand more easily. The less-used hand moves less. With regular practice for some days, both sides improve.
2. In the beginning, hold this posture only briefly for 4 to 5 days and release quickly. Then gradually increase the holding time. If held too long in the beginning, shoulder joints may become painful.
3. Do not forcefully pull or push to make the hands meet. Forcing can strain nerves and cause severe pain.
4. Many people confuse which hand goes up and which leg goes up. One rule helps: whichever leg is below, the same-side hand is below. Whichever hand is above, the same-side leg is above.
5. After enough practice, if you can clasp both hands behind near the upper back and stay 2 to 3 minutes without pain or discomfort, you may stop this posture and use that time for another posture.

Supta Vajrasana

Supta means lying down. Supta Vajrasana is the posture of lying backward from Vajrasana, so it is called by this name.
Method
1. Move toward the front part of the mat and sit in Vajrasana. Place the left palm near the left toes as shown in the photo, slowly bend to the left side, and bring the left elbow to the floor.
2. Place the right palm near the right toes, slowly bend to the right side, and bring the right elbow to the floor.
3. Then slightly lift both knees, bend backward slowly, and try to place the crown area of the head on the floor.
4. Now lower down fully so that the back, legs, hands, and head rest on the floor. The waist may remain slightly lifted in the middle. Keep the eyes fully closed.
Posture Position: Lie in the posture in full stillness, without thoughts, and keep attention on both thighs. Observe the thigh region from the knees upward. Stay as long as possible. When pain is no longer bearable, hold both ankles firmly with both hands, press the elbows to the floor, lift the head and chest to one side, and slowly come up to Vajrasana. People who are very heavy may not be able to rise this way. They can roll to one side from Supta Vajrasana, slowly stretch the legs, and then lie back.
Resting Position:
This posture feels quite difficult while holding it. You may feel like releasing quickly. While coming out, those who roll to one side and stretch their legs can take complete rest in Savasana. Those who come back step-by-step and sit in Vajrasana can rest in seated Sukhasana. Immediately after taking the rest posture, close the eyes, keep attention on the breath, and relax. Rest for 1 to 2 minutes until breathlessness and leg pain reduce.
Another Method: This posture is very beneficial for people who are overweight, even if full Supta Vajrasana is not yet possible. Even Ardha Supta Vajrasana can give them good benefit. To do this well, Vajrasana should be comfortable first. Sit in Vajrasana and slowly bend to one side, trying to place one elbow on the floor (see photo in method). If this itself is very difficult, stay there with attention on the thighs and release without placing the second elbow. If you can bend more, place the second elbow also and stay. If you still cannot place the head backward, remain in the second-photo position for some days. Keep attention there. After both elbows touch the floor, if you can bend further, arch the chest backward, slightly lift both knees, and try to place the crown on the floor.
Practice this stage for a few days as shown in the photo. After pain reduces, slowly try to place the chest down more. With gradual practice, even people with heavier bodies can gain strong benefit from this posture without problems. Release the posture and take rest as explained above.
Wrong Position: There are very few mistakes in this posture. Even if people bend to different levels, useful benefit still comes.
Benefits
1. Many women especially have very heavy thighs, and both thighs rub, causing sores and discomfort. In this posture, people with heavy body weight can reduce thigh fat effectively. Other methods often do not work this well for thigh reduction. Overall body weight may reduce by many methods, but for reducing thigh size specifically, very few postures help as much as Supta Vajrasana. If practiced regularly twice daily and held longer over time, it gives major benefit to the thighs.
2. For people without excess thigh fat, this posture makes the thighs firm and strong. It improves blood flow to the thighs and supports their health.
3. When both legs stay folded back in this position, the large sciatic nerves (sciatica) from the spine to the legs are stretched while holding. This helps reduce and prevent future sciatica-like leg pain.
4. In Supta Vajrasana, muscles of the thighs and waist get strong contraction. Because of this, muscle cells can clear waste better and gain more vital energy (prana). This gives good results from above the knees up to the lower waist region.
5. The abdominal muscles and intestines are held with firm tone in this posture, which improves their strength and function.
Note
1. People with low back pain or sciatica-related pain should avoid this posture until those symptoms reduce.
2. This is a difficult posture. Beginners should try it only after a week to ten days of initial practice, when the body becomes somewhat prepared and flexible.
3. Only those who can sit comfortably in Vajrasana for 5 to 10 minutes without pain should attempt this posture. If Vajrasana itself is painful immediately, do not try this.
4. During the first 4 to 5 days of learning, hold briefly and release early instead of staying long.
5. While entering and releasing this posture, move very carefully and slowly. Otherwise strain or pain may occur somewhere in the body.

Ustrasana

Ustra means camel. In this posture, the front body and neck curve backward like a camel shape, so it is called Ustrasana.
Method
1. Use a slightly soft mat. Kneel on both knees, keeping about one foot distance between the knees and between the feet. Keep the feet on the toes.
2. Slowly bend the waist and neck backward. First lean more to the left side and hold the left heel with the left hand.
3. Then slowly bend slightly to the right and hold the right heel with the right hand. Keep the eyes fully closed. Hold both heels firmly with both hands, bend the neck well backward, push the seat and lower waist forward, and deepen the backbend. Do not open the eyes at any point.
Posture Position: After reaching the final pose in Ustrasana, if possible push the waist further forward and keep attention on the waist and lower waist region. Strong pain may be felt in the neck area. Stay only as long as you can tolerate that sensation. When you cannot continue, release carefully. Otherwise dizziness may occur, and some people may feel body imbalance immediately after coming out. Without opening the eyes, first release one hand from the heel, bring the waist slightly forward, bend the neck completely forward, then release the second hand and slowly sit in Vajrasana.
Whenever we bend the neck and waist strongly backward, we should follow with a forward-bending posture to return the body to neutral. After that, do a rest posture. Those with neck pain, waist problems, or sciatica-like symptoms may skip the forward bend and go directly to rest. Others should do the following: after returning from Ustrasana and sitting in Vajrasana, place both hands behind the back and interlock them, exhale, and bend forward until the head touches the floor. The chest rests on the thighs. Exhale completely by the time the head reaches down. Stay below with breath retention as long as comfortable. When air is needed, rise slowly and inhale gradually as the head comes up. This is called Yoga Mudrasana. You may also keep the legs wide in Vajrasana, sit with the seat touching the floor, and bend forward in the same way. If possible, that is also good for the legs.
Resting Position: When the waist is bent deeply backward, the lungs stay stretched, so normal breathing volume becomes low while holding. Immediately after releasing Ustrasana, a long deep inhalation of more than about one liter may happen naturally. Such deep breathing gives the body more energy. From Vajrasana, release the legs and lie on the back. Keep arms and legs relaxed and rest for 2 to 3 minutes. During this rest, each breath becomes deep and fills the lungs well. The mind becomes very calm. If you place attention on the breath now, thoughts reduce and clarity increases.
Full Position: In the beginning, holding the heels in Ustrasana may be difficult. With daily practice for some time, the waist becomes used to bending backward and you may feel ready for a deeper bend. Then, instead of placing hands only on the heels, place the hands further down toward the soles in this stage and bend deeper backward, pushing the waist forward more. This increases difficulty slightly and increases benefit.
Another Method: For people with heavy upper arms, more fat around the waist, or a large abdomen, deep backbending may be difficult. Even if they bend slightly, fat around the armpit may block access to the feet. They should not skip this posture only because both hands do not reach. Try in another way: kneel with legs wider, keep feet on toes, bend to one side, place the fingertips on the top of one heel, and keep the other hand on the waist as shown in the picture. Using the hand on the waist, push the waist forward and try to bend the neck and waist backward. Hold carefully so fingers do not slip. Bend backward slowly and carefully, or there is risk of falling sideways. Stay as long as possible, then release slowly as explained earlier. Rest for 20 to 30 seconds until tiredness reduces, then do the second side in the same way. After completing both sides, do the forward-bending Yoga Mudrasana and then rest in a lying posture.
Wrong Position: For strong results, the waist should be bent backward like a clear arch, and the neck should also drop backward well. In the photo below, holding the heels is done, but the waist is not pushed forward enough, so the spine does not bend fully. If it does not bend enough, the required positive change in the spine will not come. In some people the back is very tight and does not bend well backward, so the shape looks like the photo. If tightness is less, the bend can go deeper. If effort is proper and you keep trying to bend more, it will improve to your current limit - that is the gain. Even when bending is possible, if you do not push correctly, benefit is less. In this posture, pain is usually felt more in the lower back. If that area feels too comfortable, it usually means the push is not enough. The neck also gets major benefit, so bend it back carefully.
Benefits
1. We are usually used to bending the spine forward. In this posture, deep backward bending helps the vertebrae regain natural alignment and elasticity.
2. This posture is especially useful for people with low back pain, neck pain, and sciatica-like leg pain. The three spinal regions - cervical (neck), dorsal/thoracic (mid-back), and lumbar (lower back) - become stronger with this practice. In daily life, frequent forward bending of the waist and neck compresses vertebrae forward and increases pressure on discs. That disc pressure then affects nerves. Depending on where this happens, different symptoms appear. In the neck region it may cause neck pain, arm pain, tingling, nerve pull, and headaches. In lower regions it may cause disc prolapse, sciatica pain, slipped disc, leg weakness, and related problems. A major cause is forward compression of the vertebrae. With daily Ustrasana practice twice a day, the spine bends backward, space between vertebrae improves, and pressure on discs reduces. Because of this, many of the above symptoms may reduce within 1 to 2 months. Neck pain may improve even sooner.
3. Keeping the lower waist deeply bent backward gives pressure in that region and can help reduce fat there.
4. Ustrasana reduces restrictive pressure from ribs and chest muscles on the lungs, allowing the lungs to expand better and support deeper breathing throughout the day.
5. It gives good movement and better circulation to the thyroid gland, digestive system, and pancreas gland, helping them function more healthily.
6. With daily practice, issues like low back pain, neck pain, stiffness, and pulling discomfort in the back are less likely to trouble you in life. As a preventive measure, this is an excellent posture for everyone.
Note
1. If eyes are opened immediately while coming out of Ustrasana, dizziness may occur and one may feel uncomfortable in the head. So do not open the eyes immediately.
2. For the first week, hold only 5 to 6 seconds after entering the posture and release. After adaptation, hold longer. If you bend too much and hold too long in the beginning, the waist can become strained.
3. People with neck pain or low back pain should practice carefully. After this posture, they should avoid additional forward-bending strain.
4. To get faster benefit, practice consistently twice daily. You may repeat the posture twice each session - do it once, release, rest briefly, and repeat.

Utthana Padasana

Utthana means lifting upward. In this posture, both legs are lifted and held up, so it is called Utthana Padasana. This posture is easy to start, but staying in it for longer is difficult.
Method
1. Lie on the back on a mat, bring both legs together, stretch both feet forward, interlock the fingers, and place the hands under the head.
2. Now lift both legs together about one foot above the floor without bending the knees, and hold them there. Keep the eyes closed.
Posture Position: Keep the legs still at about one foot height, place attention on the lower abdomen below the navel, and stay as long as possible. After some time, lower abdominal muscles become tired and cannot hold the leg weight. At that point, release the posture.
Resting Position:
In posture position, the abdominal muscles tighten strongly. Because of this, diaphragm movement reduces and lungs cannot expand fully. Air does not move deeply, and breathing becomes shallow. So tiredness comes quickly in this pose. Immediately after release, breathing becomes deep and long. Now lie freely on the back, bring the hands from under the head and place them on the chest, and rest. Without opening the eyes, keep attention on the breath and remain in rest for 2 to 3 minutes until tiredness reduces fully and breathing returns to normal. If another hand position feels easier, you may use that. At this moment the mind becomes calm, because deep breathing after the posture sends more oxygen to the body and brain. This gives a clear and peaceful feeling that is hard to get so easily in other ways. That is why, after finishing posture focus, shifting to breath focus gives strong benefit.
When legs are lifted slightly, the lower back vertebrae and nearby muscles carry added load and may feel fatigue. Some people feel mild discomfort near the tailbone area after release. In such cases, instead of stretching legs fully during rest, bend both knees slightly and place the feet on the floor. This allows the lower back to rest fully on the floor and recover comfortably. Everyone may choose whichever of these two rest positions suits them better.
Another Method:
People with high body weight, heavy thighs and calves, weak abdominal muscles, or a very deconditioned body may not be able to lift both legs even a little. Lifting only one leg does not give the same benefit. For such people, there is another way. Until full form is learned, they may bend both legs slightly and lift them with a small curve. Holding exactly one foot height may be hard, so they can hold at whatever height is possible. Even a short hold gives benefit. After 10 to 20 days of practice, even heavier people usually become able to do this posture better. For those with higher body and abdominal weight, the bent-knee rest position is usually more comfortable.
Wrong Position:
Even in this simple posture, some people make mistakes. The mistake does not cause major harm, but the correct target area does not receive full benefit. When both straight legs are held about one foot above the floor, their full weight falls mainly on lower-abdomen muscles and nerves. Then benefit goes to the correct area. Some people hold the legs two feet high, thinking higher is better. At higher height, weight shifts partly to upper abdomen and partly away from the intended area. Then no area gets maximum benefit. A separate posture for upper-abdomen work is done later. Utthana Padasana is specifically for the lower-abdomen region, so do not lift higher than about one foot. This is very important. Also, while holding, do not move legs up and down; movement reduces benefit.
Benefits
1. The main benefit is reducing lower-abdomen fat below the navel. Few other postures give this level of result for this area. Most people carry some lower-belly fat. Even when people try many weight-loss methods, overall body weight may reduce, but lower belly may remain. This posture works directly there and helps burn fat. Both legs together may weigh around 20 to 30 kg, and this load is carried by lower-abdomen muscles for 1 to 2 minutes. That helps reduce fat in that region. With 4 to 5 months of regular practice, the lower belly becomes flatter and tighter.
2. This posture is very helpful for women after delivery. In many women, both lower and upper abdomen become loose after childbirth. If practiced daily twice a day after about 2 to 3 months postpartum, this posture helps the stretched abdomen tighten back gradually.
3. It gives strong pressure to the rectum and large intestine. In the posture, lower abdomen becomes firm like a rock, showing deep abdominal contraction. During the hold, pressure on the bowel helps move stool forward. So this posture is very useful for people with weak bowel tone, constipation, or weak intestinal muscles. After asana practice, bowel movement may improve.
4. This posture gives special benefit to the uterus region. Because of strong abdominal contraction, strength in that area increases and may help prevent or reduce issues like uterine weakness or sagging. Normal exercise does not always create internal organ movement, but asanas create specific movement, circulation, and muscle tone in specific regions.
5. While holding this posture, lungs stay expanded; after release they contract strongly. These expansion and contraction cycles train breathing to become deeper and fuller. Over time, oxygen intake through the day can improve significantly. As oxygen circulation in the body increases, energy and capacity also improve.
6. Abdominal and intestinal muscles become stronger.
Note
1. Some people try to hold as long as possible on day one to reduce belly quickly. Then they get pain for 2 to 3 days while walking or lying down. For the first 5 to 6 days, beginners should not hold beyond about 5 to 6 seconds.
2. If breath is tightly held, you cannot stay long and benefit reduces. Practice without forcefully locking the breath. Then the lungs naturally take the required air.
3. If bowels are emptied well before practice, this posture can be held longer. If bowel movement is incomplete, pain may come early and the posture has to be released quickly.

Naukasana

When this posture is done, the body shape looks like a boat, so it is called Naukasana. Another name is Pranasana (prana asana), because it strongly affects the abdominal region where glands and organs that produce enzymes and hormones are present, and helps regulate the flow of vital energy.
Method: This posture is easy to perform, but difficult to hold.
1. Lie on your back, stretch both legs and hands straight, keep both legs together, and lift both legs about one foot from the floor without bending the knees.
2. Then, without resting the hands on the floor, hold the thighs with the hands and lift the head and chest. The back should not rest on the floor.
3. Now release the thigh hold, stretch both hands straight in the air along the upper thighs, and keep only the buttocks and waist area touching the floor while the rest of the body stays lifted. Close the eyes.
Posture Position: Keep the body and head still, and place attention on the upper abdomen above the navel. While in this posture, the upper abdomen and chest are tightly contracted, so slight shaking is natural. Staying in this pose is harder than Utthana Padasana. Remain as long as possible without excessive strain. When you feel you cannot stay any longer, slowly lean back and lie down with eyes closed.
Resting Position: Staying still in this posture even for 20 to 30 seconds is difficult. Tiredness comes very quickly. The abdomen and chest feel strongly compressed. Because the chest is held slightly lifted, breathing may reduce by around 80 to 90 percent and only minimal breathing continues, causing fatigue. Immediately after release, deep breathing starts, and the mind becomes calm quickly. After coming out, lie on your back, relax both hands and legs, place attention on the breath, and experience that clear, peaceful state without movement for 1 to 2 minutes. Stay in rest until breathing returns to normal.
In this posture also, because legs are held one foot above the floor, if anyone feels lower-back discomfort after release, they may bend the legs and rest as shown in the photo. This lets the lower back rest fully on the floor and reduces the load.
Another Method:
People with high body weight, large abdomen, or a heavy chest may not be able to rise as described above. Keeping hands unsupported in the air above the thighs is very difficult for them, though that gives maximum benefit. If full form is not possible, after lifting legs one foot high, lift the chest slightly, hold the thighs with the hands, press both elbows to the floor, and support part of chest weight through the arms while trying to hold the posture. Even with some hand support, overweight practitioners can still get full benefit. Holding so much body weight creates deep work in the upper abdomen and inner abdominal organs. Sometimes heavier people get more benefit from this Ardha Naukasana (half boat) variation than thinner people get from the full posture. After holding as long as possible, they should take the resting posture with slightly bent legs. Heavier people naturally get more breathlessness, and in this posture fatigue can be much higher, so they should rest until it fully settles.
Wrong Position:
If this posture is done incorrectly, benefit shifts away from the intended area. Normally, legs are lifted one foot, and chest and head are lifted only slightly without holding with the hands. Then leg weight falls mainly on the upper abdomen, giving the intended effect. The main purpose here is to target the upper-abdomen region. Just as Utthana Padasana gives lower-abdomen benefit, this posture gives upper-abdomen benefit, and together they support full chest and abdominal health. Some people lift too high as shown in the wrong photo. They may stay longer and look impressive, but 60 to 70 percent of benefit is lost. Lifting legs 2 to 2.5 feet is one mistake. Lifting chest too high is the second mistake. With both lifted too high, pressure on upper abdomen drops, so fatigue and pain come less and breathing feels easier. If these effort signs are missing, posture is likely wrong. Doing it as in the wrong photo does not give proper benefit.
Problems Due to Belly Fat
Many people develop abdominal fat, especially in the upper-abdomen region above the navel. Everyone likes a slim waist without belly fat, but some do not know how to reduce it and others neglect it. Even when it does not cause obvious discomfort, excess belly fat harms breathing and diaphragm movement. Breathing cannot reach the bottom of the lungs, so lung filling reduces and breathing remains shallow. Then breaths per minute increase beyond ideal levels. This gradually reduces vital energy in the body and leads to tiredness, breathlessness, and dullness. If breath rate stays high, longevity and capacity can reduce. So it is best to act now. We have learned good postures for belly reduction - Utthana Padasana and Naukasana. If practiced carefully twice daily, a large part of belly fat may reduce in 4 to 5 months.
Benefits
1. Naukasana mainly reduces upper-abdomen fat and makes the abdominal wall thinner and firmer. In this posture, legs and chest are both lifted slightly, and the upper-abdomen muscles carry that load. Even with short holding time, the result for belly reduction can be excellent. Some practitioners have reduced abdominal thickness by 5 to 6 inches in 3 to 4 months with daily practice. Very few postures give such strong benefit for upper-abdomen fat.
2. This posture has a good effect on the pancreas gland in the abdomen. While holding the tight posture, pressure reaches this gland and influences insulin-producing cells. It acts like a strong massage to the gland. For people with diabetes, it may help reduce blood glucose levels to some extent.
3. Organs that support digestion - liver, gall bladder, small intestine, and stomach - also receive useful pressure in this posture. Their function can improve, digestive juices can increase, and issues like indigestion and low appetite may reduce.
4. After childbirth, many women have a stretched abdomen. This posture is very helpful for reducing that stretch. It burns fat, tightens the abdomen, and strengthens abdominal muscles. It can help restore the body closer to its pre-pregnancy state. Practicing twice daily is beneficial.
Note
1. This posture usually cannot be held long. So, to get more benefit, do one round, rest fully until fatigue settles, and then do a second round.
2. In the beginning, do it briefly and release quickly until the body adapts. Once there is no pain and the body is comfortable, you may hold longer or do a second round.

Sarvangasana

Each asana gives a special effect on certain organs and gives specific benefits. Not every asana works on all organs. But Sarvangasana works on almost all parts and organs of the body, so it is called Sarvangasana. The name itself shows how valuable this posture is. Sirsasana is not possible for everyone, and if done wrongly it can cause harm. Sarvangasana, when practiced correctly, gives high benefit with lower risk.
Method
1. First lie on your back with a folded mat under the head area, legs stretched, and hands by the sides. Then lift both legs together without bending the knees, keeping the feet pointing upward in a straight line.
2. Now bend the legs slightly, lift the waist in one smooth motion, and place both palms on the lower back for support so the body does not fall. Knees come near the face.
3. If the hands are not placed properly on the waist, you may slip down. As shown in the photo, keep four fingers supporting the back and place the thumb on the side waist. This gives a firm grip.
4. Hold the waist firmly and move the legs upward so they become more straight from the knees onward.
5. Now adjust the legs and waist so the body becomes fully aligned. Try to keep the body from shoulders to feet in a straight 90 degree vertical line. Stretch both feet upward, close the eyes, and keep the body steady.
Posture Position: After entering Sarvangasana, the body does not stay perfectly still immediately. Hands may slip, so adjust and re-hold them firmly when needed. The waist may also bend, so keep correcting it back to straight. From the time you enter the full pose, observe the circulation changes in the body with awareness. Feel changes from feet up to the seat region. If Sarvangasana is held only 1 to 2 minutes like other poses, full benefit does not come. Hold as long as you can with control. After 2 to 3 minutes, you may notice tingling in the feet and reduced circulation in the legs. Around abdomen and chest, you may feel increased blood flow; eyes and face may look red. While holding Sarvangasana, chest movement is limited and breathing becomes shallow, especially due to the neck bend. If held 5 to 6 minutes, legs may become very numb. From throat to lower abdomen, skin may look pink-red due to increased circulation. If you can stay 5 to 10 minutes safely, circulation changes are stronger, and whole-body benefit is greater. After holding enough, those who can do Halasana may move to it from this position. Those who cannot do Halasana should release Sarvangasana slowly. People with good alignment, previous practice, and enough flexibility may try Halasana. Let us see how to go from Sarvangasana to Halasana.
Halasana: "Hala" means plough. In this posture, the body shape resembles a plough, so it is called Halasana.
From Sarvangasana, slowly lower the raised legs, bend the knees, bring both knees to either side of the face, and place the feet on the floor behind the head. Then release the hands from the waist and stretch them on the floor. After that, slowly straighten both knees and move the feet farther away, until legs are in one line from feet to hips. Stay in this position as long as comfortable. Then slowly bend the legs, lower the chest, and return to normal lying position.
After releasing Sarvangasana, legs often have tingling. So, without taking rest first, doing a forward bend like Paschimottanasana gives good effect. Rest may be taken after Paschimottanasana. Likewise, if you moved from Sarvangasana to Halasana, after releasing Halasana, do Paschimottanasana first and then go to rest.
Another Method: For people with high body weight, Sarvangasana may be difficult. If the body allows slightly, they can still try. In heavier people, the body may not come fully vertical, but whatever level is reached still gives benefit. If unable to do independently, take help from another person to lift the legs, then hold the waist with hands and try to support leg weight. Holding the hip-waist region as shown in the photo makes it easier. Stay as long as possible and return slowly. The chance of serious mistakes in this posture is low when done carefully.
Benefits
1. This posture sends more blood to the thyroid gland and helps balance thyroid hormone variations. So many thyroid-related problems can reduce, and others may prevent future thyroid issues.
2. Because the body is inverted, blood supply to pituitary and pineal glands in the head improves, increasing their function. Since pituitary controls many hormonal systems, Sarvangasana can support all endocrine glands.
3. In children with slow growth, this posture may support better gland function and hormone production, helping growth.
4. For people with varicose veins, this posture helps leg veins gain better tone and carry blood more effectively. In varicose problems, impure blood remains in the legs longer instead of moving easily toward the heart, causing heaviness and burning. Such people should practice Sarvangasana regularly twice daily if suitable. If daytime leg discomfort is high, they may skip full Sarvangasana and simply raise legs as in the first step for 2 to 3 minutes; this gives relief. It can be repeated 5 to 6 times a day.
5. Feet and legs are farthest from the heart, so circulation there is naturally slower. In physically active people, work itself helps circulation. In inactive people, poor circulation causes tingling, burning, and reduced sensation, especially after age 40 to 50. Medicines alone often do not help much. Sarvangasana improves active circulation in feet and legs. Daily practice may help prevent vessel blockage or narrowing in the toes and feet.
6. This posture gives increased blood flow to the brain while holding, so more oxygen reaches the brain and mental activity improves. Students who practice daily may improve memory and thinking ability.
7. During Sarvangasana, neck and shoulders carry body weight, making the neck region stronger and helping prevent neck-related pain.
8. Good blood flow comes to the throat region. Even with heavy speaking, throat fatigue, hoarseness, and throat pain may reduce. Throat muscles become stronger and support blood movement upward to the head.
9. Daily Sarvangasana helps bones, muscles, nerves, and related systems come under better control and function more efficiently, making the body more flexible.
10. Even for physically active people, inner organ movement may remain limited. Sarvangasana gives broad internal movement, better circulation, and better vital energy to many organs, helping them function more actively.
Note:
1. People with neck pain, low back pain, heart problems, or high blood pressure should not do Sarvangasana.
2. Beginners should first practice other asanas well for about 1 to 2 months, then start Sarvangasana.
3. If body weight creates major difficulty and the posture is not possible now, postpone Sarvangasana for some time.
4. People above 60 years may fully skip this posture if they feel difficulty doing it.

Paschimottanasana

Paschima means the back side of the body. In this posture we lift and bend the back side forward, so it is called Paschimottanasana. Another name is Paschima Tanasana, because it helps direct vital energy through the Sushumna path.
Method
1. Sit with both legs stretched straight in line, keeping knees unbent and legs pressed to the floor.
2. Bend forward from the waist and hold both feet or toes with both hands. Keep the knees unbent.
3. Slowly bend the head toward the knees as if trying to kiss them, and bend until both elbows move toward the floor. Keep both legs pressed down and not lifting. Keep eyes closed.
Posture Position: Keep the legs from lifting, keep the head close to the knees, and place attention on the region below the knees and in the thighs. Stay as long as possible. This position is difficult. Leg nerves are stretched strongly and may feel like sharp pulling. If knees are bent even a little, benefit reduces. So keep legs still and straight as long as possible. If severe cramp-like pain comes in abdomen or side body, release immediately and rest. In this posture, the diaphragm remains mostly compressed, so air flow is low and breathlessness can increase. Stay as long as manageable. When pain is not bearable or more air is needed, rise slowly. On rising, deep inhalation naturally happens. If done right after Sarvangasana without rest, fatigue can be high, so extra rest is useful.
Resting Position: Because Paschimottanasana and Sarvangasana can cause strong fatigue, lie on the back, relax legs and hands freely, and keep attention on the breath to regain calmness. Since breathing was restricted during Paschimottanasana, deep breaths now come strongly and calm the mind. Take full rest for 2 to 3 minutes. The next postures are also difficult and tiring, so full recovery here is important.
Another Method: People with large abdomen or high body weight may not be able to do full Paschimottanasana. Seeing full posture, many feel afraid and think they cannot do asanas. They may not know that even partial bending gives benefit. The truth is: wherever your body can bend, benefit starts there. So do not lose confidence if you cannot touch knees with the head. Bend as far as possible while trying not to lift the knees. Even if you can bend only like the photo below, benefit can still be good. The key point is not how far you bend, but how steadily you stay there. If you bend a little and come up immediately, benefit is very low. Both thin and heavy people may find this posture difficult. Thin people may feel very tight like a stick and cannot bend forward easily. This is one of the hardest postures for most people. But with steady effort and focused holding, all can gain good results. Take rest after practice.
For some, touching both knees is very difficult. For those who cannot bend forward even a little, a simpler version can be used first. Practice with one leg for a few days, then move to two-leg practice after some improvement.
1. Keep one leg fully straight. Bend the second leg fully and place it as shown in the photo.
2. Now bend forward and hold the foot of the straight leg with both hands, ensuring the straight leg does not lift.
3. Without allowing the straight-leg knee to lift even slightly, try to bring the head toward that knee and move elbows toward the floor.
Bend as far as possible without lifting the leg and stay briefly. When you cannot continue, release fully, sit up, and take about one minute rest. Then switch legs and repeat on the other side. Practice both sides separately and then take full rest.
Wrong Position:
Many people make mistakes in this posture. Their only focus is touching knees with the head. To make that happen, they lift the knees toward the head. As shown in the photo, this is wrong. In this posture, bending any amount gives benefit, but lifting legs makes benefit almost zero. When knees bend, nerves from spine to legs are not stretched properly, and abdominal muscles do not contract properly. Then the expected body changes do not happen. So do not focus on kissing the knees. Focus on keeping legs straight and stable.
Benefits
1. The spine stretches to its maximum possible range. Compared to normal seated length, spinal extension in this posture can increase significantly.
2. Muscles on both sides of the spine become stronger and circulation improves.
3. Nerves between the waist and pelvic region become stronger. As a result, organs supplied by these nerves can function better.
4. Nerves running from back into legs are deeply stretched. During deep bending, strong pull in thigh nerves may be felt. Especially the large sciatic nerve gets stretched, helping reduce future risk of sciatica-like pain or leg control issues.
5. While holding the posture, muscles below the knee and in the thighs are strongly stretched and contracted, which improves their strength.
6. In deep bending, abdominal muscles are strongly compressed. This pressure helps reduce fat and makes the abdomen thinner. It also helps tighten a stretched belly.
7. This posture is useful for people with diabetes as well. Forward bending increases pressure on the pancreas gland and supports better cell activity, which may help improve insulin production to some extent.
8. It gives strong pressure on the large intestine (rectal region). This can push retained stool forward and improve bowel pushing strength. That helps reduce constipation and supports smoother bowel movement.
9. Muscles and nerves involved in breathing, and the lungs, gain stronger contraction ability in this posture. This can help improve breathing volume and reduce breath rate over time.
10. Uterus, urinary bladder, liver, stomach, intestines, spleen, and related organs receive useful movement and improved circulation in this posture.
Note
1. People with low back pain, sciatica-like pain, or neck pain should not do this posture.
2. Do not force by asking someone to press strongly from above to make you touch knees. Forced pressure can cause major problems.
3. In the first few days, doing too much can cause pain severe enough to affect walking. Calf muscles may become very tight. For 5 to 6 days, hold briefly and release. Increase duration gradually after adaptation.
4. If you cannot hold the feet while bending, or if there is large distance, do not lose hope. This posture may take months for many people. With consistent practice, gradual change comes.
5. People with liver disorders, high blood pressure, or heart disease are better off skipping this posture.

Bhujangasana

Bhujanga means snake. In this posture, the body looks like a cobra lifting its hood, so it is called Bhujangasana. Just as a snake can turn quickly forward and upward, this posture helps us make the waist and spine more flexible and easier to move.
Method:
1. Lie on the stomach with the head area on the mat. Place both palms near the navel on either side of the waist, with fingers touching as shown in the photo and with minimal palm pressure.
2. Press both elbows inward and keep them in line with the chest. While lifting into the final posture, the distance between elbows should remain the same and should not widen.
3. Keeping elbows pressed inward, join both feet and stretch them fully. Try to lift head and chest upward from near the navel. Bend the neck fully backward. Keep eyes fully closed.
Posture Position: After lifting head and chest as much as possible from the navel area, keep attention from the waist upward to the neck and stay without movement as long as possible. Since the chest is bent backward, lungs, ribs, and chest muscles remain expanded while holding the pose. So only about 10 to 15 percent normal airflow may happen in the posture. This can cause breath hunger and fatigue. There may be strong squeezing pain in the back and waist. Staying more than 10 to 20 seconds may be difficult at first; with practice, you can gradually stay 1 to 2 minutes. Most people can do this posture, but it gives strong fatigue and strong results in short time. The longer you hold steadily, the better the benefit. Results depend on time spent in proper posture position. So stay until the body clearly asks to release. To come out, lower the chest slowly and lie down on the stomach. Do not open the eyes immediately.
Resting Position: Lie face-down, leave legs and hands relaxed, and turn the face to one side as shown in the picture.
Keep attention on the breath and experience the calm state. Many people feel deeper mental peace in the resting phase after Bhujangasana than in many other poses. Immediately after releasing posture position, about 20 to 25 deep breaths may come naturally, spreading vital energy through the body. In rest, the mind can enter a near-thought-free still state. Stay in this experience for 2 to 3 minutes, or until breath returns to normal. Without proper rest, the next posture cannot be held well. For the four prone postures in this sequence, slightly longer rest is especially useful.
Another Method:
Some people have weak back muscles and low spinal strength, so they cannot do the main method well. For them, and for people with higher body weight, an easier version is useful. In the main method, placing palms too low near the navel can feel very hard. So in this variation, move the hands slightly forward. Instead of only fingers touching, place the full palms on the floor. Keep both palms a little ahead of the navel area. This gives some hand support while lifting chest, making the posture easier while still beneficial. Lie prone, place hands slightly forward, stretch feet fully, press both elbows inward, and lift chest and head with partial hand support to the final possible position. Stop there and keep eyes closed.
Wrong Position:
Many people make mistakes in this posture. The result depends strongly on hand placement. A common error is placing hands too far forward. Then chest weight from the navel upward is lifted mainly by the arms. After lifting, arms keep bearing most of the load, and back and waist do less work. If hands carry the load, spinal benefit drops. A snake lifts without external support. Similarly, true Bhujangasana should not depend heavily on the hands. If lifted fully by arms, the core purpose changes. When hands are placed below the navel area as instructed, hand assistance is only about 10 to 15 percent, and about 85 percent benefit goes to back and waist. In the easier method with hands slightly forward, hand help may be around 30 to 40 percent, leaving 60 to 70 percent spinal benefit. But in the wrong form with hands far forward, hand help may become 80 to 85 percent, and back benefit may reduce to around 15 to 20 percent. So adjust hand position based on your current capacity and patience. If severe back pain exists, you may begin with a more supported version and then gradually move hands backward as pain improves. This posture helps reduce body weight and waist fat; for that goal, more backward hand placement gives better effect. One more key point: if elbows are not pressed inward while lifting chest, the body will rise on hand support alone. To increase benefits, press both elbows inward while lifting from the waist and back. Even if you cannot place hands perfectly now, keep trying to move them backward gradually.
Benefits
1. Bhujangasana gives strong benefit for low back pain, neck pain, sciatica-like leg pain, and related spinal issues. For many people with such conditions, this posture is like a blessing. While holding, backward bending of waist and neck increases spacing between vertebrae and reduces pressure on discs. It helps prevent nerve compression between vertebrae. For some people, regular practice has reduced symptoms enough to avoid surgical intervention for certain back and neck problems. It is best to practice twice daily. Twice-daily practice can give faster relief.
2. Today many people suffer from some neck or back issue. Medicines alone cannot fully prevent these problems, and often do not solve them once severe. Physical labor alone also does not fully protect against them. Asana practice is a strong solution. Instead of suffering later, daily practice can prevent many neck and back issues in life.
3. This is also an effective posture for weight reduction. Because it is demanding to perform and hold, energy use is high during practice, helping weight loss. Practicing twice daily can speed results.

5. It gives useful pressure to kidneys and may help prevent circulation-related disturbances in them.
6. This posture supports lung function and helps the body adapt toward deeper breathing throughout the day, which improves overall energy.

Sarpasana

When this posture is done, the body looks like a snake raising its hood. Compared to Bhujangasana, there is a small change in hand position. Let us learn that change here.
Method:
1. Lie on the stomach with the head on the mat. Keep both legs close together and feet stretched well. Hold the lower buttock area with the fingers of both hands.
2. Pull the buttock area firmly with the fingers, lift the chest upward from near the navel, and bend the head backward. Lift chest and neck as high as possible and hold there.
Posture Position: After lifting the front body well from the navel region, hold there and place attention on the lower waist and neck region. Since hands are not resting on the floor in this posture, it is more difficult than Bhujangasana. Holding longer is especially hard. Staying even 20 to 30 seconds may require high effort. Stay as long as possible, then when you cannot continue, release and lie down on the stomach. Do not open the eyes immediately.
Resting Position:
The body will feel strongly tired. Stay face-down, keep legs and hands fully relaxed, and take full rest. During this time, place attention on the breath and experience calmness. The lungs expand even more than in Bhujangasana, so fatigue may come faster. In posture position, back and waist get deep contraction and can feel quite painful. Many people feel strong relief when they release this posture, showing how intense it is. It is one of the postures that gives high benefit in short time. In resting position, deep breathing restores energy and removes fatigue. Stay in rest until the body returns to normal. There are very few mistake patterns in this posture; even if done in different levels, full benefit can still come.

Shalabhasana

Shalabha means grasshopper. In this posture, the front body stays lower while the back body lifts high, resembling a grasshopper. So it is called Shalabhasana.
Method
1. Lie on the stomach with the head on the mat. Close both fists and place them under the upper thighs (as in the second photo). Keep both legs together and feet stretched. Place the chin on the floor. Keep eyes closed.
2. Tighten the waist and legs, and try to lift both legs together upward without bending the knees. Lift as high as possible and hold.
Posture Position: In Shalabhasana, hold the legs up and keep attention on the buttock area and lower waist (tailbone area). While holding, legs should not move and should not drop down. Some trembling in the legs is natural. Keeping legs up here requires strong effort. The body from neck to feet becomes tightly engaged. Only with that full engagement do legs rise well. Breathing may become shallow, and fatigue can appear. In this posture, the mind usually stops wandering and stays focused. Hold as long as possible, then lower when no longer manageable.
Resting Position:
Without opening the eyes, stay lying down. Open and relax the hands, keep legs free from tension, and place attention on the breath. Many people feel strong fatigue in this posture. Deep breathing in rest brings more oxygen and removes that fatigue. In this resting phase, the mind can become very still and thought-free. This shows how much asana supports mental stillness. Stay until the body fully returns to normal. Keep eyes closed during rest.
Another Method:
People with heavy thighs and calves may find this difficult. If body weight is high, lifting both legs needs more strength in muscles and nerves. If this is not possible, start with easier variations and gradually build toward full posture. If both legs cannot be lifted, lift one leg at a time. For strong sciatica-like symptoms, single-leg holds may be especially useful.
Another variation: if straight-leg lifting is very low, and in heavy thighs the thigh still stays on the floor, benefits may be limited. In such cases, lifting with slight knee bend can still give good benefit. For heavier practitioners, a small knee bend may work better than not lifting at all. Doing some version is important. There is no harmful mistake pattern here when practiced carefully; different versions can still give useful results.

Dhanurasana

After doing this posture, the body looks like a drawn bow, so it is called Dhanurasana. The held legs and hands act like the bowstring, and the rest of the body forms the bow shape. It is considered a combined form of Bhujangasana and Shalabhasana.
Method
1. Lie on the stomach. Keep about one foot distance between legs. Bend both knees backward toward the buttocks. Reach one hand back and hold one ankle, then hold the other ankle with the other hand.
2. Hold both ankles firmly, kick both legs upward and backward, and lift chest and neck up. Keep chest steady and legs controlled. Close the eyes.
Posture Position: Once the bow shape is formed, stay still and place attention on whichever region from calves to neck feels more strain. Breathing remains somewhat better here than in some other prone poses, so longer holds are possible. In the four prone postures, this can often be held longest. Avoid swinging like a cradle; that may feel easy but reduces benefit. Asana means stable holding. Stay firm until true fatigue appears, then slowly lower legs, release hands, and lie down.
Resting Position:
Leave hands and legs fully relaxed, lie on the stomach, turn the neck to one side, and rest with attention on breath. Since hold time can be longer in this posture, fatigue can be higher, so take 2 to 3 minutes of rest. Deep breathing in rest brings calm and recovery. After full recovery, prepare for Savasana.
Another Method:
People with heavy upper arms, calves, or thighs may struggle to hold both ankles. They can use a one-side variation instead of skipping the posture. Lie prone with legs slightly apart. Bend one knee, place the opposite hand slightly in front for support, lift chest and neck, and hold ankle or foot of the bent leg. If needed, take help to reach the leg. Kick that leg backward while lifting chest from near the navel. Hold steadily, rest, then repeat on the other side.
Wrong Position:
A common mistake is holding ankles but not lifting the chest. If chest does not rise, benefit is very low. To lift chest properly, keep arms firm and unbent, hold ankles strongly, and kick legs backward actively. If still difficult, someone can help lift the legs first until you find proper chest lift. Then hold independently. Remember: strong backward leg kick is the key.
Benefits
1. It gives strong relief for low back and neck pain and helps prevent recurrence with regular practice.
2. It reduces excess calf fat and gives better shape through strong pressure in the hold.
3. It supports weight reduction. Though slightly less intense than some poses, energy use is still high and useful for fat loss.
4. Lifting front and back together creates strong pressure at the waist center, improving circulation there and helping waist slimming.
5. It improves the relationship between vertebrae, waist muscles, and nerves, making the waist more controllable and flexible.
6. It gives useful effect on uterus and urinary bladder region, and may help women with related weakness or downward sagging tendencies.
Note
1. Do Savasana only after fatigue has clearly reduced.
2. Beginners should practice lightly for 2 to 3 days first, then gradually increase hold duration.
3. In all four prone postures, ensure bladder is not full before practice.
4. These four prone postures are especially important and should not be neglected in daily practice.

Savasana

After completing all asanas, Savasana is essential for full physical and mental recovery. Many people assume simply lying down with eyes closed is enough rest, but involuntary muscle tension often remains. Without releasing that tension, full rest does not happen.
Through daily stress, both body and mind stay overactive. Even sleep may not be truly restful for many people. That is why people often wake up tired. Savasana teaches conscious rest. With regular yoga, pranayama, and meditation, the body and mind can stay calmer even during difficult situations.
During daytime work, muscles and nerves get fatigued. Mental pressure adds more tension. Stress states like fear, anger, and anxiety overactivate adrenal glands, tighten vessels and muscles, and burden the heart. Chronic stress reduces immunity and worsens many disorders, including digestive issues. Learning deep rest is therefore necessary for a healthy life.
Observe children or animals while they sleep - that is true rest. Adults often keep thinking even while resting. In Savasana, we reduce thought flow by placing awareness on body and breath. As muscles become still, neural signaling related to voluntary movement reduces, and deep relaxation increases. Cells of muscles, nerves, and organs recover. The brain also rests deeply. In this state, mind becomes clearer and quieter. Savasana can provide profound recovery; 5 to 10 minutes is usually very beneficial.
Savasana: In this posture, the body lies on the ground like a still corpse, without movement or muscular tightening. Hence the name Savasana.
Method: Lie on your back on a mat. Keep legs slightly apart and stretched. Keep hands slightly away from the body, stretched and relaxed. Palms face upward. Keep eyes closed.
Now observe whether each body part is touching the floor or not. This is a good method to bring attention onto the body.
First observe whether the buttock area is touching the floor fully. If tightened, release it completely so weight settles down. Then observe the right heel touching the floor for a few seconds, and then the left heel.
Now observe right hand, right leg, left hand, left leg, back, both shoulders, and back of head one by one for a few seconds each. Then feel the whole body resting on the floor. Feel right leg becoming heavy and still, then left leg, then right hand and left hand similarly becoming heavy and sinking. If fists tighten, open them gently. Keep shoulders fully loose. Do not wrinkle the forehead. Keep jaw relaxed. Keep mouth closed. Let all muscles remain without movement. If thoughts arise, do not fight them; bring attention back to body and breath. Stay this way for 5 to 10 minutes, then slowly open eyes, bend hands and legs, and get up.

14. Surya Namaskaralu (Sun Salutations)

The Sun gives light and warmth to the world. Without the Sun, life on earth cannot continue. For this reason, people have honored the Sun from ancient times. The Sun is directly visible to us, and supports our life in many ways. So expressing gratitude to the Sun is part of our tradition.
In older times, Sun salutations were practiced with mantra and fixed devotional postures. Modern practice is more movement-based and helps prepare the body. Though not the same as static asana practice, Sun Salutations are very useful before asana and pranayama. They reduce stiffness in joints and muscles, remove sleep heaviness, and energize body and mind.
1. Surya Namaskara has 12 positions, often connected with 12 zodiac signs.
2. A special feature is that breathing can be coordinated naturally in each position.
3. Traditionally, each position is done with one mantra. Vibrations from mantra recitation can support mental focus and inner strength.
The 12 traditional mantra names used in sequence are:
1. Om Mitraya Namah (friend of all)
2. Om Ravaye Namah (the shining one)
3. Om Suryaya Namah (source of beautiful light)
4. Om Bhanave Namah (radiant intelligence)
5. Om Khagaya Namah (mover in the sky)
6. Om Pushne Namah (giver of strength)
7. Om Hiranyagarbhaya Namah (golden cosmic womb)
8. Om Marichaye Namah (lord of rays)
9. Om Adityaya Namah (son of Aditi)
10. Om Savitre Namah (stimulator, life-giver)
11. Om Arkaya Namah (source of energy)
12. Om Bhaskaraya Namah (giver of brilliance)
First learn the 12 movements with proper breathing. Then add the mantras. In final form, movement, breath, and mantra flow together. Chant aloud when possible; otherwise chant mentally.
Completing all 12 positions is one round. Based on capacity, you may do 2 to 12 rounds. If possible, 12 rounds in the morning is very good. Do not force beyond fatigue. Practice on an empty stomach or at least 3 hours after food. Men and women of all ages may practice as suitable.
How To Do Surya Namaskara
1. Pranamasana:
Stand upright with some gap between feet and palms joined in prayer. Chant: Om Mitraya Namah.
2. Hasta Uttanasana:
Raise joined hands up and bend waist and neck backward slightly. Chant: Om Ravaye Namah.
3. Pada Hastasana:
Bring hands to floor, keep knees straight, and bend toward knees. Chant: Om Suryaya Namah.
4. Ashwa Sanchalanasana:
Stretch left leg back, keep right knee bent between hands, chest supported through hands and front leg, neck bent back. Chant: Om Bhanave Namah.
5. Parvatasana:
Take right leg back as well, lift hips up, keep head in and look toward navel, legs straight as possible. Chant: Om Khagaya Namah.
6. Ashtanga Namaskarasana:
Lower chest between hands in controlled way. Beginners may keep knees and chest lightly supported. Chant: Om Pushne Namah.
7. Bhujangasana:
Lift chest with hand support and bend neck fully back. Chant: Om Hiranyagarbhaya Namah.
8. Parvatasana:
From there, lift hips up again and look toward navel. Chant: Om Marichaye Namah.
9. Ashwa Sanchalanasana:
Bring left leg forward between hands, right leg back, neck back. Chant: Om Adityaya Namah.
10. Pada Hastasana:
Bring right leg forward between hands, keep legs straight, bend toward knees. Chant: Om Savitre Namah.
11. Hasta Uttanasana:
Rise up, raise hands overhead, bend chest and neck back. Chant: Om Arkaya Namah.
12. Pranamasana:
Bring palms to chest and stand in prayer. Chant: Om Bhaskaraya Namah.
Note
1. Those with neck or low-back problems should avoid Surya Namaskara.
2. If you include both, do Surya Namaskara first, then pranayama and asanas.
3. One round is all 12 positions with 12 mantras in sequence. 12 rounds is considered excellent.
4. On days without time for full practice, you may do only Surya Namaskara rounds.
5. Even though positions resemble asanas, this is more movement-oriented and not a replacement for full static asana practice.
6. Like exercise, this can produce sweat and fatigue. Doing both asana and Surya Namaskara regularly is ideal.
Expressing gratitude to the Sun daily is a meaningful tradition. With devotion and disciplined practice, these methods support both health and joy in life.
